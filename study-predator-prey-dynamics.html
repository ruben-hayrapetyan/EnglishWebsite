The chapter in 16 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · What are predators, prey, and infradian rhythms?

Predators have traits for hunting

  • Predators are animals that naturally hunt other animals for food. One hunts for survival while the other hides.
  • Exposure to different environments and genetic mutations led animals to evolve different features and adaptations over time.
  • Snakes and scorpions use neurotoxic venoms to subdue prey, cheetahs have body structures for hunting faster, and crocodiles use ambush tactics.

1 · What are predators, prey, and infradian rhythms?

Prey have traits for survival

  • Prey are animals that are caught and killed by predators for food.
  • Skunks have foul-smelling chemicals, and turtles have shells that prevent physical damage from predators.
  • Nocturnal animals are specialized in avoidance strategies to hide away from others.

1 · What are predators, prey, and infradian rhythms?

Infradian rhythms link to the cycle

  • Infradian rhythms are biological cycles that last longer than a 24-hour period.
  • The evolutionary changes and genetic mutations both species undergo count as infradian rhythms, because they are continual cycles longer than 24 hours.
  • Seasonal variation causes behavioral or physiological changes in predators and prey, which adds to the fluctuation in their populations.

1 · What are predators, prey, and infradian rhythms?

Patterns let scientists classify rhythms

  • Because populations change in a cycle, scientists can collect data and find patterns in how species respond to change.
  • Bears, rats and turtles all hibernate in winter to conserve energy when food and resources are scarce, so hibernation is classed as an infradian rhythm.
  • Infradian rhythms are also vital processes that maintain long-term stability in the ecosystem.

2 · The predator and prey population cycle in the ecosystem

Snowshoe hares and lynxes

  • Predator–prey dynamics: predator and prey population sizes are not constant over time and sometimes fluctuate in opposite directions.
  • Snowshoe hares (Lepus americanus) are herbivorous mammals of North America's boreal forests whose populations cycle in eight- to eleven-year periods.
  • They run up to 27 miles per hour and breed fast: two to three litters a year, with one to nine young per litter.
  • Hares are the primary food of the Canadian lynx (Lynx canadensis), so the two species' cycles are closely linked.

2 · The predator and prey population cycle in the ecosystem

How the cycle turns

  • Hares increase thanks to high reproductive rates, which gives lynxes more food for growth and reproduction, so lynxes rise.
  • More lynxes eat more hares, so the hare population falls.
  • Fewer hares means food scarcity for lynxes, so lynxes gradually decline. The pattern repeats as an infradian cycle.
  • Most predator and prey populations follow this same generalized pattern.

2 · The predator and prey population cycle in the ecosystem

Top-down and bottom-up control

  • Top-down control: a population is mainly limited by predators. Rising predators put pressure on prey and prey numbers fall.
  • Bottom-up control: a population depends on the availability of resources, the bottom level of the food chain.
  • A prey peak means abundant food and a rise in predators; a prey trough means less food and a drop in predators.

3 · What factors drive or maintain the periodic infradian population cycles?

The pattern isn't the whole story

  • Prey and predators often resist population change through evolved adaptations, environmental changes and habitat structures.
  • Both evolve through natural selection: favorable traits let some organisms survive longer than less well-adapted ones.
  • Four kinds of factor help maintain the cycle: abiotic, resource and habitat, community, and biological and evolutionary.

4 · Abiotic factors

Rain and aphids

  • Abiotic factors are the non-living parts of the environment, such as sunlight, water and temperature.
  • Berg et al. (2008) found rain removed aphids from wheat shoots and ears by 57% and 25%, and independently reduced aphid populations by 27%.
  • Heavy rain also knocks aphids to the ground, where predatory arthropods live, so predation rises too.
  • Prey decline is not solely due to predation: extreme weather matters.

4 · Abiotic factors

Rainfall cuts both ways

  • Drought can weaken prey and make them vulnerable: predators rise in the short run, then drop in the long run as prey become scarce (Morin et al., 2021).
  • High rainfall makes vegetation dense, so herbivorous prey grow and reproduce more.
  • Dense vegetation helps lions, leopards and lynx ambush prey, but also helps prey conceal themselves.
  • Rainfall doesn't universally benefit either side; it depends on how each responds.

5 · Resource and habitat factors

Food drives the snowshoe hare

  • Resources in the ecosystem can include food, mates and decomposers.
  • Krebs et al. ran an 8-year field experiment in the Yukon, manipulating food supply, predator availability and soil nutrients.
  • Fertilizer increased plant biomass but did not significantly change hare density.
  • A constant supply of supplemental food tripled the hare population, so food availability is the paramount factor for prey growth.

5 · Resource and habitat factors

Habitat as a refuge

  • A habitat is the place where a group of related organisms lives, and is also commonly used as an area of refuge.
  • Lennox et al. (2025) studied sculpin preying on salmon eggs, which lie under riverbed substrates.
  • Smaller substrates make a denser protective layer and shrink the gaps predators can reach through.
  • So habitat structure strongly affects salmon egg and sculpin populations.

6 · Community factors

Predator switching

  • A food web shows "what-eats-what" in an ecological community. Predators often hunt more than one prey.
  • The chapter's example: tigers hunt zebras, but also wildebeests, buffaloes and impalas.
  • Predator switching gives other prey species time to grow before they are hunted, allowing different prey to coexist (Archibald et al., 2023).
  • This stabilizes the food web and the cycle by preventing extinction.

7 · Biological and evolutionary factors

Evolved traits

  • Animals can evolve traits that make them better adapted and less prone to being hunted than other species.
  • Chameleons change their skin color to match the background, confusing predators and surviving longer.

7 · Biological and evolutionary factors

Stress lowers hare reproduction

  • Sheriff et al. (2009) found that maternal snowshoe hares under predation risk secreted the stress hormone glucocorticoids.
  • Their reproductive rate fell while they were under pressure from predators.
  • Higher stress was linked to lower reproduction, which helps explain the steep fall in hares when predators surge.

8 · Conclusion

A continuous process

  • The predator–prey cycle is complicated yet continuous, and prevalent in all ecosystems.
  • Population sizes fluctuate for reasons ranging from environmental factors to molecular biological factors.
  • Predators keep hunting and prey keep hiding, which drives more evolutionary change.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Predators
An animal that naturally hunts other animals for food.
Prey
An animal that is caught and killed by another (predator) for food.
Infradian rhythms
A biological cycle that occurs for longer than a 24-hour period.
Predator–prey dynamics
Natural occurrences when the population sizes of predators and prey in a community are not constant over time, and sometimes fluctuate in opposite directions.
Top-down control
When a population is mainly limited by predators, which exert forces on the population size.
Bottom-up control
When a population is heavily dependent on the availability of resources or the bottom level of the food chain.
Natural selection
The process in which certain favorable traits allow species to survive longer compared to other less well-adapted organisms in the environment.
Abiotic factors
The non-living components of the environment that influence the growth of organisms, such as sunlight, water and temperature.
Habitat
The place in which a group of related organisms lives; also commonly used as the area of refuge.
Substrates
Layers of materials covering the bottom of a riverbed.
Food web
An interconnected diagram that shows "what-eats-what" in an ecological community.
Predator switching
When predators switch their target prey, which allows for the coexistence between different prey types by giving other prey species time to grow in population size.
Glucocorticoids
A type of stress hormone that the endocrine system of maternal snowshoe hares secreted when they were under predation risk.

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 · Define predators, prey and infradian rhythms, and explain how infradian rhythms connect to the predator–prey relationship.

Predators are animals that naturally hunt other animals for food, and prey are animals caught and killed by predators. One hunts for survival while the other hides, and each has evolved traits for that role, like venom or shells. Infradian rhythms are biological cycles longer than 24 hours. They connect to the relationship because the evolutionary changes both species undergo are continual cycles of that length, seasonal variation changes their behavior and physiology, and their populations rise and fall in multi-year cycles that help keep the ecosystem stable.

LO2 · Walk through one full turn of the snowshoe hare and lynx cycle, and say where top-down and bottom-up control appear.

Hares increase because they reproduce fast. More hares means more food for lynxes, so lynxes rise. More lynxes eat more hares, so hares fall; that's top-down control, predators limiting prey. With fewer hares, lynxes face food scarcity and gradually decline; that's bottom-up control, a population limited by its resources. Then the cycle repeats, with hare populations returning to their original level in eight to eleven years.

LO3 · Name the four types of factors the chapter uses, and give one example that shows how each is different.

Abiotic factors are non-living parts of the environment, like rain washing aphids off wheat. Resource and habitat factors are what species need to live and where they find refuge, like supplemental food tripling snowshoe hares or small substrates protecting salmon eggs. Community factors are relationships between species in a food web, like predators switching between prey. Biological and evolutionary factors are traits and body processes, like chameleon color change or stress hormones lowering hare reproduction. They matter because predators and prey often resist the simple cycle through adaptations, environmental changes and habitat structures.

LO4 · Does rainfall help predators or prey? Use the chapter's evidence to argue your answer.

It depends. Heavy rain hurt aphids directly, cutting their populations by 27%, and knocked them to the ground where predatory arthropods ate more of them. Drought can weaken prey, which helps predators in the short run, but predators then drop in the long run as prey run short. High rainfall makes vegetation dense, which feeds herbivorous prey and hides them, but also gives predators cover for ambushes. So rainfall doesn't universally benefit either side; it depends on how each species responds.

LO5 · How do Krebs et al.'s hare experiment and Lennox et al.'s salmon egg study show that resources and habitat shape predator–prey populations?

In the Yukon, fertilizer grew more plant biomass but didn't significantly change hare density, while a constant supply of supplemental food tripled the hare population. So food availability is the paramount factor for hare growth. For salmon eggs, the habitat is a refuge: smaller substrates on the riverbed make a denser protective layer with smaller gaps, so sculpin reach fewer eggs. In both cases what the prey can eat, or where it can hide, changes population levels, not predators alone.

LO6 · Explain how predator switching keeps a food web stable.

A food web shows what eats what, and most predators have more than one prey; tigers, in the chapter's example, hunt zebras, wildebeests, buffaloes and impalas. When a predator switches its target prey, the species it stops hunting gets time to grow its population before the predator returns to it. That lets different prey types coexist, and prevents any one species from being hunted to extinction, which stabilizes the food web and the predator–prey cycle.

LO7 · Give one evolutionary and one molecular biological factor from the chapter, and explain how each changes prey populations.

Evolutionary: chameleons evolved the ability to change their skin color to match the background, which confuses predators and lets them survive longer. Traits like this make a species less prone to being hunted. Molecular: Sheriff et al. found that maternal snowshoe hares under predation risk secreted glucocorticoids, a stress hormone, and their reproductive rate fell. Higher stress meant lower reproduction, which helps explain why hare numbers fall so steeply when predators surge.
