Breeding and migration

How internal yearly clocks and daylength tell animals when to breed and when and where to migrate, and how climate change and artificial light throw that timing off.

The chapter in 17 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Circannual rhythms and synchronization

A clock that repeats once a year

  • Many animals must track time through the year: breeding in warm seasons, migrating across continents.
  • They rely on circannual rhythms: internal biological clocks that repeat roughly once a year.
  • The question: are seasonal behaviors just passive responses to the weather, or driven by a clock that runs without environmental cues?

1 · Circannual rhythms and synchronization

Stonechats in constant conditions

  • African stonechats were kept at a steady temperature and an unchanging 12 hours of light and 12 hours of dark (Dittami & Gwinner, 1985).
  • With seasonal cues removed, they still molted and grew their testes on schedule.
  • The cycles lasted roughly 10–11 months, not 365 days, so they would drift from the real seasons.
  • Conclusion: the cycles are generated endogenously, not set by the seasons.

1 · Circannual rhythms and synchronization

Daylength keeps the clock in step

  • Because internal clocks aren't exactly a year long, animals use zeitgebers, environmental cues, to synchronize.
  • The most reliable cue is photoperiodism: daylength differs between seasons (Gwinner, 2003).
  • Melatonin is made only at night. Long summer days mean a shorter secretion; short winter days mean a longer one (Paul et al., 2008).

1 · Circannual rhythms and synchronization

Duration, not amount

  • In Siberian hamsters, the daily duration of melatonin secretion, not its total amount, signals day length (Carter & Goldman, 1983).
  • In spring, shorter nights push the duration below a threshold.
  • The brain registers spring and resets the circannual clock, which helps set the breeding timeline (Paul et al., 2008).

2 · Internal timing of seasonal breeding

Two ways to time breeding

  • Offspring should be born in the warm months of spring, but animals don't all keep time the same way.
  • A circannual clock cycles roughly once a year on its own.
  • A seasonal interval timer works like an hourglass: one cycle lasts several months, is started by a seasonal cue, and can't repeat on its own (Paul et al., 2008; Wood & Loudon, 2014).

2 · Internal timing of seasonal breeding

The Siberian hamster's interval timer

  • Under the short days of winter, male Siberian hamsters' gonads shrink and reproductive activity drops.
  • Several months later the gonads grow again, even with daylight unchanged in the lab.
  • So the hamsters aren't simply following outside conditions: they are "governed by a process with hourglass properties designated an interval timer" (Paul et al., 2008, p. 342).

2 · Internal timing of seasonal breeding

Flipping the hourglass

  • The gonads responding to winter is like flipping the hourglass: the "sand" starts to run.
  • When the sand runs out months later, gonad growth is triggered automatically.
  • The timer won't reset itself: it needs exposure to long days to start another cycle.

2 · Internal timing of seasonal breeding

The ground squirrel's true circannual clock

  • Golden-mantled ground squirrels hibernate for months in dark burrows, with no daylight cues.
  • Kept in constant lab conditions for years, they still hibernated in autumn and winter and were active in spring and summer (Pengelley & Fisher, 1957).
  • Both systems time reproduction, but only a circannual clock repeats its cycle indefinitely under constant conditions.

3 · Melatonin secretion and breeding time

From melatonin to the gonads

  • Melatonin helps synchronize both circannual clocks and interval timers.
  • It affects the release of gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GNRH).
  • GNRH influences pituitary hormones that activate the gonads, and reproductive activity increases (Wood & Loudon, 2014).

3 · Melatonin secretion and breeding time

Pregnancy length sets the season

  • Small mammals with short pregnancies, like Siberian hamsters, can begin breeding in spring.
  • Sheep and deer have longer pregnancies, so they mate in autumn to give birth in spring.
  • Long days activate reproduction in hamsters; short days activate it in sheep (Wood & Loudon, 2014).
  • Breeding time depends on both melatonin duration and the time pregnancy needs.

4 · Migration timing and navigation

Knowing when to leave

  • Bad timing can mean failing to escape winter, arriving late at breeding sites or missing seasonal food.
  • Songbirds are active by day but migrate at night to avoid predators.
  • Kept at constant daylength and temperature, they still fluttered restlessly at night during migration periods: migratory restlessness.
  • This shows readiness to migrate and an internal circannual clock; daylength changes keep it accurate (Gwinner, 2003).

4 · Migration timing and navigation

A Sun compass needs a clock

  • The Sun moves across the sky, so birds must change their angle to it to hold one direction.
  • Their circadian clocks tell them the time of day, so they can adjust the angle (Mouritsen, 2018).
  • Flying south in the Northern Hemisphere: Sun on the left in the morning, ahead at noon, on the right in the afternoon.
  • Clock-shifted pigeons chose different directions under sunny skies (Guilford & Taylor, 2014).

4 · Migration timing and navigation

Three stages of navigation

  • Birds may also use stars or the Earth's magnetic field (Mouritsen, 2018).
  • First, broad celestial or magnetic cues get them to the correct region.
  • Then landmarks and olfactory differences narrow down the home area.
  • Finally, specific sights or smells locate a specific site.

5 · Modern environmental threats

When the seasons shift

  • Internal clocks keep animals on a similar schedule every year, which may not match a changing environment.
  • When climate change shifts the timing of seasonal events, such as breeding or plant growth, it is a phenological shift.
  • Species adjust at different rates, creating a mismatch between food supply and when animals arrive.

5 · Modern environmental threats

Pied flycatchers arrive too late

  • Warmer springs in the Netherlands made caterpillars reproduce earlier, so the birds' food peak came earlier (Both et al., 2006).
  • The flycatchers weren't there before migrating, so they didn't notice the warm spring or advance their arrival.
  • Populations fell by about 90% where food peaked earliest, and by about 10% where it peaked latest.
  • Climate change can harm animals by disrupting seasonal timing, not only through heat.

5 · Modern environmental threats

Artificial light at night

  • Night-migrating songbirds rely on celestial and magnetic cues.
  • Strong artificial light at night (ALAN) can distract birds from celestial cues.
  • Lit-up city buildings disorient them: they circle skyscrapers or collide fatally.
  • Hundreds of millions of birds die each year from collisions with buildings (Van Doren et al., 2017).

5 · Modern environmental threats

Switching off the lights works

  • Van Doren et al. (2017) used radar at New York's "Tribute in Light" memorial, with the beams on and off, over seven nights in seven years.
  • With the lights on, birds gathered and called intensely; peak density was more than 20 times the surrounding baseline every year.
  • With the lights off, the birds dispersed within minutes.
  • Reducing nighttime lighting during migration periods can protect birds from collisions.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Circannual rhythm
An internal biological clock that repeats roughly once a year. Can be kept constantly without outside factors.
Photoperiodism
The ability of animals to measure season changes with daylength.
Melatonin
A hormone produced during darkness by the pineal gland. Animals use the duration of it to track daylength and sense the time of year.
Migration restlessness
Hyperactive behavior shown during animals' normal migration period.
Phenology
The timing of seasonal life-cycle events in creatures in response to environmental conditions.
Zeitgeber
An external environmental cue that synchronizes an organism's internal biological clock.
Diurnal
Active primarily during the daytime.
Nocturnal
Active primarily during the nighttime.
Interval timer
A seasonal timer that functions similarly to an hourglass: one cycle lasts several months rather than a whole year, each cycle is activated by a seasonal cue, and it cannot repeat the cycle on its own.
Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GNRH)
A hormone whose release melatonin affects. It influences hormones from the pituitary gland that activate the gonads.
Phenological shift
The change when climate change shifts the timing of seasonal events, such as breeding or plant growth.
Artificial light at night (ALAN)
Strong human-made light that can distract migrating birds' use of celestial cues to navigate.

  1. Stonechats kept at a steady temperature and an unchanging 12 hours of light and 12 hours of dark still molted and grew their testes, but on cycles of about 10–11 months. What does this show?

    1. Their seasonal changes are passive responses to the weather
    2. The yearly cycle is generated inside the bird, but it isn't exactly a year long, so it needs outside cues to stay in step
    3. The 12-hour light cycle acted as the seasonal cue that triggered molting
    4. Their internal clock runs exactly 365 days, so they never need environmental cues
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The weather and seasons were held constant, yet the cycles continued, so they can't be passive responses.
    2. Right: the cycles persisted without seasonal cues (endogenous), and their 10–11 month length means they would drift without synchronization.
    3. The light cycle never changed, so it carried no information about the season.
    4. The cycles lasted 10–11 months, not 365 days, so they would drift away from the natural seasons.

    Dittami and Gwinner (1985) removed seasonal cues, and the birds still went through their yearly changes: the rhythm is endogenous. Because it ran shorter than a year, the chapter explains that animals rely on zeitgebers such as daylength to keep it synchronized.

  2. According to the Siberian hamster experiment, what tells an animal's brain how long the day is?

    1. The total amount of melatonin made each day
    2. Melatonin made during the daytime
    3. Daily changes in temperature
    4. How long melatonin secretion lasts each night
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The experiment showed that the total amount is not the key signal; the duration is.
    2. Melatonin is synthesized only during the night, not during the day.
    3. The chapter names daylength (photoperiod), read through melatonin, as the most reliable cue, not temperature.
    4. Right: the daily duration of melatonin secretion is the signal that identifies day length.

    Carter and Goldman (1983) showed in Siberian hamsters that duration, rather than total amount, of melatonin secretion identifies day length. Long summer days give a short secretion, and short winter days a long one.

  3. Nights get shorter as spring arrives. What does the chapter say happens next?

    1. Melatonin secretion becomes short enough to drop below a threshold, the brain registers spring, and the circannual clock is reset
    2. Melatonin secretion lasts longer, which signals that spring has come
    3. The body stops making melatonin until the following winter
    4. The circannual clock starts for the first time and runs only until autumn
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: crossing that threshold tells the brain spring has arrived and resets the circannual clock.
    2. That's backwards: shorter nights mean a shorter melatonin secretion. Longer secretion goes with winter.
    3. Melatonin is still made every night; what changes is how long the secretion lasts.
    4. A circannual clock runs all year on its own; spring cues reset it rather than start it.

    When spring nights shorten, melatonin duration drops below a critical point. The brain then recognizes spring and resets the circannual clock, keeping it synchronized with the seasons and helping set the breeding timeline (Paul et al., 2008).

  4. Siberian hamsters begin breeding in spring, but sheep and deer begin mating in autumn. Why the difference?

    4. Hamsters have no internal timing and simply breed whenever it is warm
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Both use daylength through melatonin; short days are what activate reproduction in sheep.
    2. The goal for both is birth in the warm spring; the chapter says nothing about pregnancy needing cold.
    3. Right: the time needed for pregnancy shifts when mating must start so that birth lands in spring.
    4. Hamsters use an interval timer; their gonads regrow on an internal schedule even when daylength doesn't change.

    The chapter concludes that the breeding timeline depends on both melatonin duration, set by daylength, and the time needed for pregnancy. Short pregnancies allow spring breeding; long ones require autumn mating.

  5. Which order matches the chain the chapter describes, from the daylength signal to more reproductive activity?

    1. Gonads, then GNRH, then pituitary hormones, then melatonin
    2. Melatonin, then GNRH, then pituitary hormones, then gonads

    4. GNRH, then melatonin, then gonads, with no pituitary step
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. This runs the chain in reverse: the gonads are the end point, activated by the other signals.
    2. Right: melatonin affects GNRH release, GNRH influences pituitary hormones, and those activate the gonads.
    3. Melatonin comes first, as the signal of daylength; the pituitary acts later, on the gonads.
    4. Melatonin acts on GNRH, not the other way round, and the pituitary is the step that activates the gonads.

    Melatonin affects the release of gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GNRH). GNRH then influences hormones from the pituitary gland that activate the gonads, and reproductive activity increases (Wood & Loudon, 2014).

    In the chapter's hourglass model, the shift between short and long days is like turning over an hourglass. Just as you must flip an hourglass to start again, the interval timer needs long days to reset (Paul et al., 2008).

  9. Golden-mantled ground squirrels kept in constant lab conditions for years still hibernated in autumn and winter. How does this differ from the hamster?

    3. The chapter says they hibernate in dark burrows for months without access to daylight cues.
    4. Both can time reproduction, but the hamster's timer needs long days to restart.

    Pengelley and Fisher (1957) kept the squirrels in constant conditions for years, and their yearly pattern continued. The chapter uses them to contrast a self-repeating circannual clock with the hamster's hourglass-like interval timer.

    Answer: B

  12. When researchers shifted pigeons' circadian clocks, the birds chose different directions on sunny days. What does this tell us?

LO1 · How do animals keep track of the time of year, and why do they need both an internal clock and outside cues?

Animals have circannual rhythms, internal clocks that repeat roughly once a year. Stonechats kept in constant conditions still molted and grew their testes on schedule, so the rhythm is endogenous. But it ran about 10–11 months, not a year, so it would drift. Animals keep it in step with zeitgebers, mainly daylength. They measure daylength by how long melatonin is secreted each night: when spring nights shorten, the duration drops below a threshold and the clock is reset.

LO2 · Explain how daylength and pregnancy length together decide when an animal breeds.

Animals time breeding so their young are born in the warm spring. Daylength sets the duration of melatonin secretion, which affects GNRH release. GNRH influences pituitary hormones that activate the gonads. Siberian hamsters have short pregnancies, so long days switch on reproduction and they breed in spring. Sheep and deer have longer pregnancies, so short days switch on reproduction and they mate in autumn.

LO3 · Use the hourglass model to explain the Siberian hamster's breeding cycle, and how it differs from the ground squirrel's.

Months later the sand runs out and the gonads grow again, even if daylength hasn't changed. The timer can't restart itself; it needs long days to be reset. The golden-mantled ground squirrel has a true circannual clock: in constant conditions for years it still hibernated in autumn and winter, so its cycle repeats indefinitely on its own.

Navigation has three stages: broad cues to reach the region, landmarks and smells to narrow down the home area, and specific sights or smells to find the exact site.

The chapter's evidence suggests it works well. Van Doren et al. used radar at the Tribute in Light memorial over seven nights in seven years. With the beams on, birds gathered and called intensely, and peak density was more than 20 times the surrounding baseline every year. When the beams were switched off, the birds dispersed within minutes. So reducing nighttime lighting during migratory periods can effectively protect birds from collisions.
