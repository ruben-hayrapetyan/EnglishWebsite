The chapter in 18 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Overview

Hunger runs on feedback loops

  • Most people eat when hungry and stop when full, but the biology behind appetite is complicated.
  • The body relies on many feedback loops to keep energy homeostasis.
  • Meal timing is mostly independent of the main circadian rhythm. It depends more on hormones, time of day and meal schedule.

2 · The essential appetite regulators

GLP-1: a fast "food is being digested" signal

  • GLP-1 is a peptide made by L-cells in the small intestine, mainly the ileum, as food is digested.
  • It binds GLP-1R in the pancreas, which releases insulin to lower blood sugar, and in the brain, which slows stomach emptying and increases satiety.
  • The enzyme DPP-IV breaks down about 80% of it before it reaches the hepatic portal vein, and the rest within minutes.
  • Ozempic, a diabetes drug, is a GLP-1-like molecule that does not break down as fast.

2 · The essential appetite regulators

Leptin: the long-term "don't eat" signal

  • Because GLP-1 is short-lived, the body also needs long-term energy regulation.
  • Leptin is made by fat cells when stored fat is high. It is not released right after a meal.
  • It is anorexigenic: in the arcuate nucleus it activates POMC neurons and deactivates AgRP/NPY neurons.
  • When someone is dangerously underfed, leptin plummets and the body cuts back on non-essential uses of energy, such as reproduction.

2 · The essential appetite regulators

Ghrelin: the hunger signal

  • Ghrelin stimulates hunger, usually when the stomach is empty or around a regular meal time.
  • Unlike GLP-1 and leptin, it is orexigenic (it stimulates appetite).
  • It is secreted by PD/D1 cells, endocrine cells in the stomach.
  • It does nearly the opposite of leptin: it deactivates POMC neurons and activates AgRP/NPY neurons.

2 · The essential appetite regulators

Meal times train hunger

  • Eating at regular times causes anticipatory hunger, partly from ghrelin spikes around usual meal times.
  • Beyond the master clock, meal timing is strongly entrained by peripheral clocks.
  • Wehrens et al. kept 10 men on a constant sleep/wake cycle and shifted their meals 5 hours later.
  • Melatonin and cortisol stayed the same, but their daily insulin peak shifted by about 5 hours to match the meals.

3 · Hormones and hunger signaling

A clock in the stomach

  • Ghrelin levels rise and fall with time since the last meal and time of day. It acts on receptors in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus.
  • Ghrelin-releasing cells contain the clock proteins PER1 and PER2, which are also found in the SCN.
  • With regular meals, PER1 and PER2 peak in the evening, when ghrelin bottoms out, and bottom out in the afternoon, when ghrelin spikes.
  • They work mostly independently of CLOCK and the SCN, so regular meal times matter more than sunlight or sleep schedule.

3 · Hormones and hunger signaling

Why dieting can make you ravenous

  • Leptin is made by white adipose tissue as fat storage increases.
  • When people chronically undereat or try to lose weight, fat tissue shrinks and leptin levels plummet.
  • The result is intense hunger that works to maintain energy homeostasis.
  • With enough fat storage, leptin secretion rises and appetite is more controlled.

3 · Hormones and hunger signaling

GLP-1 and the gut microbiome

  • GLP-1 secretion follows a circadian rhythm, though the timekeeping mechanism in L-cells is not well known.
  • GLP-1 tends to peak after morning and afternoon meals. L-cells are less active toward the evening, so secretion after a meal is lower then.
  • The gut microbes Akkermansia and Lactobacillus increase GLP-1 production.
  • Dysbiosis, a gut microbiome imbalance, is associated with less GLP-1.

3 · Hormones and hunger signaling

The food entrainable oscillator

  • Rats on a strict feeding schedule peak in activity and energy use 2-4 hours before a meal: food-anticipatory activity (FAA).
  • Deprived of food for days, they kept the same rhythm, so routine meal timing, not food itself, drives FAA.
  • Constant light deprivation or an SCN lesion had no effect on FAA, so the FEO runs independently of the master clock.
  • The FEO is a peripheral clock, but its function and location are still unknown.

3 · Hormones and hunger signaling

Master clock or peripheral clocks?

  • Most links between these hormones and specific circadian rhythms are not fully understood and are under heavy research.
  • Some hormones sit on a spectrum between the master clock and the peripheral clocks.
  • Cortisol sits at the master clock end: it can regulate peripheral clocks, but meal timing barely affects it.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

Metabolism follows a daily cycle

  • Metabolism is the breakdown of food and drink into energy, and it relies heavily on the circadian rhythm.
  • When active, the body breaks energy down (catabolism). At rest, it builds energy up (anabolism).
  • A 2018 CDC survey found 66.3% of adults had drunk alcohol in the past year, and 5.1% were heavy drinkers.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

The liver puts ethanol first

  • The liver breaks down food and drink, mainly glucose, into ATP for the body.
  • When ethanol arrives, the liver breaks it down first, and glucose conversion is put on hold.
  • So ATP production is blocked.
  • The liver processes about one drink per hour, hence the one-drink-per-hour rule.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

Four stages of aerobic respiration

  • Glycolysis, in the cytosol, splits glucose into 2 pyruvate, making 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
  • Pyruvate oxidation, in the mitochondria, turns pyruvate into acetyl CoA.
  • The citric acid cycle releases 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 2 CO2.
  • The electron transport chain uses the NADH and FADH2 to make 32 ATP.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

Clock and metabolism steer each other

  • Through the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the organs, the circadian rhythm triggers catabolic pathways in the daytime and anabolic pathways in the evening.
  • In turn, the clock senses the molecules metabolism uses and alters clock proteins to follow them.
  • For example, as more NAD+ byproducts are made, a clock protein is modified: a cell's energy alters its molecular clock.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

How the body breaks down ethanol

  • In the cytosol, the enzyme ADH turns ethanol into acetaldehyde, a toxic carcinogen, turning one NAD+ into NADH.
  • In the mitochondria, ALDH turns acetaldehyde into acetic acid, turning another NAD+ into NADH.
  • Acetic acid is less toxic. It is split into CO2 and H2O, which leave through urine, breath and sweat.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

Too much NADH stalls the cycle

  • Aerobic respiration prefers a one-to-one ratio of NADH to NAD+, but breaking down ethanol makes more NADH.
  • High NADH tells the cell it has plenty of energy, so the citric acid cycle is inhibited, even though too little ATP is made.
  • Unused acetyl CoA goes to fatty acid synthesis, glycolysis slows and blood sugar can drop dangerously.
  • The electron transport chain cannot make enough ATP, leading to organ damage, muscle fatigue and lethargy.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

Alcohol shifts metabolism's clock

  • With molecule breakdown slowed, the liver can no longer turn energy creation up in active hours and down at rest, and its internal clock shifts.
  • Alcohol lowers melatonin secretion and alters cortisol patterns, shifting the sleep-wake cycle that metabolism's timing depends on.
  • It shuts down thermoregulatory control. Enzymes work less efficiently away from body temperature, slowing metabolism and signaling core clock proteins.

4 · Alcohol’s effect on the metabolic cycle

Long-term damage: liver, fat and gut

  • An out-of-phase liver clock makes the liver susceptible to tissue injury, scarring and even cancer.
  • Fat builds up because fat is not broken down and excess acetyl CoA becomes fatty acids. This can cause alcohol-induced liver disease.
  • A weak liver clock weakens other organs: a damaged gut lining lets toxins in ("leaky gut").

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Ghrelin
A peptide hormone responsible for regulating energy intake and expenditure. Ghrelin synthesis in the body rises as the stomach empties and energy availability is low. The peptide also inhibits insulin secretion and promotes glucagon in order to increase blood sugar to provide energy to cells.
Leptin
A peptide hormone responsible for regulating energy intake and expenditure. Leptin is produced by adipose (fat) cells when levels of stored fat are high. Leptin also promotes usage of fat for energy.
Gut microbiome
The ecosystem of microbial life (prokaryotes such as bacteria and archaea) which can consist of both beneficial and pathogenic microorganisms inside of the digestive tract.
Zeitgeber
Any entrainment factor for the circadian clock. Examples include light exposure, food intake, and wake/sleep times.
Shift work
The process of working at abnormal times such as night shifts or early morning shifts.
Metabolism
The process of breaking down energy in a cycle.
ATP
A molecule that stores and transports energy in the cell.
GLP-1
A peptide produced by L-cells in the intestinal lining when food is being digested. It signals the pancreas to produce more insulin when blood sugar is high and signals the central nervous system that food is being digested.
Anorexigenic
Appetite suppressing, as leptin is.
Orexigenic
Appetite stimulating, as ghrelin is.
Dysbiosis
A gut microbiome imbalance, associated with a decrease in GLP-1 production.
Food-anticipatory activity (FAA)
A peak in physical activity and energy expenditure 2-4 hours before a meal in animals on a strict feeding schedule.
Food entrainable oscillator (FEO)
A peripheral clock behind food-anticipatory activity that runs independently of the master clock in the SCN. Its function and location in the body are still unknown.
Catabolism
The part of the metabolic cycle in which energy is broken down, which occurs when someone is active.
Anabolism
The part of the metabolic cycle in which energy is made, which occurs when one is at rest.
Aerobic respiration
A catabolic process that breaks down nutrients (mainly the preferred sugar, glucose) into energy, in four stages: glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain.
NADH
The molecule created by adding an electron to NAD+. Both act as electron carriers that can provide energy for reactions.
Acetaldehyde
The toxic chemical that ethanol is broken down into by the enzyme ADH. It is a known carcinogen capable of harming protein and DNA.

  1. In the Wehrens et al. study, 10 men kept a constant sleep/wake cycle while every meal was moved 5 hours later. What happened?

    1. Melatonin and cortisol shifted by about 5 hours, but insulin stayed the same
    2. The daily insulin peak shifted by about 5 hours, while melatonin and cortisol stayed the same
    3. Nothing shifted, because meal timing is set by the master clock
    4. Melatonin, cortisol and insulin all shifted by about 5 hours together
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: this reverses the result. Melatonin and cortisol are master clock markers, and the sleep schedule that sets them did not change.
    2. Right: insulin followed the meals, while the master clock markers stayed put.
    3. Wrong: the insulin peak did move, so meal timing is not simply set by the master clock.
    4. Wrong: the master clock markers stayed the same; only the meal-linked rhythm moved.

    The chapter uses this study to show that meal timing is highly entrained by peripheral clocks: changing when you eat moves meal-linked rhythms like insulin without moving the master clock's melatonin and cortisol.

  2. Which statement best matches the chapter's view of how meal timing relates to the main circadian rhythm?

    1. Meal timing is set directly by light reaching the SCN
    2. Meal timing has nothing to do with hormones
    3. Meal timing is mostly independent of the main circadian rhythm and depends more on hormones, time of day and meal schedule
    4. Meal timing is decided only by when you go to sleep and wake up
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: the chapter says the hunger rhythm depends less on sunlight than on regular meal times.
    2. Wrong: hormones such as ghrelin, leptin and GLP-1 are central to the hunger cycle.
    3. Right: this is the chapter's own summary in the overview.
    4. Wrong: a constant sleep schedule matters less than eating at regular times for a regular hunger rhythm.

    The chapter separates the master clock (melatonin, cortisol, sleep) from peripheral clocks that track food, and says meal timing leans on the peripheral side.

  3. A student wants a steady daily hunger rhythm. Based on how the stomach's PER1 and PER2 proteins work, what should they focus on?

    1. Getting more sunlight each morning
    2. Going to bed at exactly the same time each night
    3. Eating whenever they happen to feel hungry
    4. Eating their meals at regular times each day
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: light is a zeitgeber for the master clock, but the stomach's clock proteins work mostly independently of the SCN.
    2. Wrong: the chapter says a constant sleep schedule is less important here than regular meal times.
    3. Wrong: irregular eating removes the routine that entrains anticipatory hunger.
    4. Right: food intake at regular times is the zeitgeber the stomach's clock follows.

    PER1 and PER2 in the ghrelin-releasing cells work mostly independently of CLOCK and the SCN, so "exposure to sunlight and having a constant sleep schedule is less important than eating at regular times for a regular hunger rhythm."

  4. Rats on a strict feeding schedule became active 2-4 hours before meals. When food was withheld for days, the activity kept appearing at the old meal times. What does this show?

    1. The routine of meal timing, not the food itself, entrains the activity
    2. Food in the stomach is needed each day to trigger the activity
    3. The activity is driven by light reaching the master clock
    4. The rats were responding to falling leptin from starvation
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: the rhythm persisted with no food, so the learned schedule is what drives it.
    2. Wrong: there was no food for days, yet the activity continued.
    3. Wrong: the chapter reports that light deprivation and SCN lesions had no effect on this activity.
    4. Wrong: falling leptin would not produce activity timed to the old meal schedule.

    This is food-anticipatory activity (FAA). The chapter concludes "routine meal timing rather than food itself is the reason behind FAA," which makes meal routine a zeitgeber for the food entrainable oscillator.

  5. What does ghrelin do when it reaches the arcuate nucleus?

    1. Activates POMC neurons and deactivates AgRP/NPY neurons, sending a "don't eat" signal
    2. Activates both POMC and AgRP/NPY neurons equally
    3. Deactivates POMC neurons and activates AgRP/NPY neurons, stimulating hunger
    4. Signals the pancreas to release insulin
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: that is what leptin does, the appetite-suppressing hormone.
    2. Wrong: ghrelin pushes the two neuron types in opposite directions.
    3. Right: ghrelin is orexigenic and has the near opposite effect of leptin.
    4. Wrong: that is GLP-1's job; the chapter's glossary says ghrelin actually inhibits insulin secretion.

    Leptin and ghrelin act on the same neurons in opposite ways: leptin turns on appetite-suppressing POMC neurons, and ghrelin turns on appetite-stimulating AgRP/NPY neurons.

  6. Where along the digestive tract is GLP-1 made, and what does it do in the pancreas?

    1. In the stomach, when it is empty; it blocks insulin to raise blood sugar
    2. In L-cells of the small intestine, mainly the ileum, during digestion; it triggers insulin release to lower blood sugar
    3. In fat cells, when fat stores are high; it releases glucagon
    4. In the liver, from ethanol; it produces ATP
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: the empty stomach makes ghrelin, which the glossary says inhibits insulin.
    2. Right: L-cells release GLP-1 as food is digested, and it binds GLP-1R in the pancreas to release insulin.
    3. Wrong: fat cells make leptin, the long-term signal.
    4. Wrong: the liver does process ethanol, but it does not make GLP-1.

    Each hunger signal comes from a different part of the body: ghrelin from the stomach, GLP-1 from the ileum and leptin from fat tissue (Figure 2).

  7. Someone has been chronically undereating to lose weight, and their fat stores have shrunk. What does the chapter predict about their hunger?

    1. Leptin plummets, so they become intensely hungry
    2. Leptin rises, so their appetite becomes easier to control
    3. Ghrelin stops being made, so they stop feeling hungry
    4. Nothing changes, because leptin only responds to individual meals
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: leptin is made by fat tissue, so it falls as fat shrinks, and intense hunger follows.
    2. Wrong: that describes someone with sufficient fat storage.
    3. Wrong: an emptier stomach is when ghrelin is secreted, not when it stops.
    4. Wrong: leptin is not produced right after a meal; it tracks fat stores over the long run.

    The chapter calls leptin a negative feedback loop for long-term energy: when fat stores shrink, leptin drops and hunger rises "to maintain energy homeostasis."

  8. A shift worker eats their biggest meal late in the evening. Based on GLP-1's daily rhythm, what would you predict?

    1. GLP-1 after that meal will be higher than after a morning meal
    2. GLP-1 will not be released at all, because it only responds to light
    3. Leptin will spike right after the meal to replace GLP-1
    4. GLP-1 after that meal will be relatively lower, because L-cells are less active toward the evening
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: GLP-1 tends to peak after morning and afternoon meals, not evening ones.
    2. Wrong: GLP-1 is released when food is being digested; its rhythm lowers it but does not switch it off.
    3. Wrong: leptin is not produced instantly after a meal.
    4. Right: the chapter says secretion is relatively lower after an evening meal.

    GLP-1 secretion follows a circadian rhythm in the gut. Eating at an unusual time of day meets a less active L-cell, so the usual fullness and insulin signal is weaker.

  9. Rodents on a constant feeding schedule kept their food-anticipatory activity after constant light deprivation or an SCN lesion. How should you interpret this?

    1. The FEO is located in the SCN
    2. The FEO runs independently of the master clock
    3. Light is the main zeitgeber for meal timing
    4. The experiment shows exactly where the FEO is in the body
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: removing the SCN did not remove the activity, so the FEO can't depend on it.
    2. Right: both treatments stop the master clock working, and FAA was unaffected.
    3. Wrong: taking light away had no effect, so light is not what drives this rhythm.
    4. Wrong: the chapter says the FEO's function and location are still unknown.

    Both treatments "ensure that the master clock doesn't function," yet FAA continued. That is why the chapter calls the FEO a peripheral clock.

  10. Compare the human meal-shift study (Wehrens et al.) with the rat feeding studies. What do both results have in common?

    1. In both, light was the factor that shifted the rhythm
    2. In both, the master clock markers moved to match the meals
    3. In both, a meal-linked rhythm followed the feeding schedule while the master clock was unchanged or not needed
    4. In both, the rhythm disappeared as soon as the schedule changed
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: in both cases, the feeding schedule was the zeitgeber.
    2. Wrong: in humans, melatonin and cortisol stayed the same.
    3. Right: the human insulin peak followed the meals with no change in melatonin or cortisol, and rat FAA continued without a working SCN.
    4. Wrong: in rats, the rhythm even persisted for days without food.

    The measures differ (insulin in humans, activity before meals in rats), but the pattern is the same: food timing entrains peripheral clocks separately from the master clock.

  11. Which list puts the stages of aerobic respiration in the right order?

    1. Glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, citric acid cycle, electron transport chain
    2. Pyruvate oxidation, glycolysis, electron transport chain, citric acid cycle
    3. Citric acid cycle, glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, electron transport chain
    4. Glycolysis, citric acid cycle, electron transport chain, pyruvate oxidation
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: glucose is split first, then pyruvate becomes acetyl CoA, which enters the cycle, and the chain comes last.
    2. Wrong: pyruvate oxidation needs the pyruvate that glycolysis makes, so it can't come first.
    3. Wrong: the citric acid cycle needs acetyl CoA from pyruvate oxidation.
    4. Wrong: pyruvate oxidation comes second, not last.

    Each stage uses what the one before it makes: glucose, then pyruvate, then acetyl CoA, then the NADH and FADH2 the electron transport chain uses.

  12. Which stage of aerobic respiration makes by far the most ATP, according to the chapter?

    1. Glycolysis, in the cytosol
    2. Pyruvate oxidation, in the mitochondria
    3. The citric acid cycle
    4. The electron transport chain
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: glycolysis makes 2 ATP.
    2. Wrong: pyruvate oxidation turns pyruvate into acetyl CoA and releases NADH, not most of the ATP.
    3. Wrong: the citric acid cycle releases 2 ATP, along with NADH and FADH2.
    4. Right: it uses the NADH and FADH2 from earlier stages to make 32 ATP.

    The earlier stages mostly load up electron carriers; the electron transport chain cashes them in for "a substantial amount of ATP energy."

  13. You plot a liver's energy production across 24 hours on a normal day and on a heavy-drinking day. What difference would the chapter predict?

    1. The drinking day shows higher energy production all day
    2. The drinking day loses the usual rise in active hours and fall at rest, because the liver's clock shifts
    3. The two curves are the same, because the liver's clock only follows light
    4. The drinking day shows a sharper day-night difference than normal
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: alcohol halts glucose breakdown, so energy creation slows rather than rises.
    2. Right: with molecule breakdown slowed, the liver's internal clock is shifted.
    3. Wrong: the chapter says metabolism itself feeds back on the clock, so alcohol changes it.
    4. Wrong: the chapter describes a disrupted rhythm, not a stronger one.

    Normally "the liver can turn up energy creation during active hours and slow it down during rest." High NADH makes the citric acid cycle act as if energy is abundant, glucose breakdown halts and the liver's clock shifts (Davis et al., 2018).

  14. Breaking down ethanol raises NADH compared with NAD+. How does that change the metabolic system?

    1. It speeds up the citric acid cycle, so more ATP is made
    2. It has no effect, because NADH and NAD+ are interchangeable
    3. It signals plenty of energy, so the citric acid cycle is inhibited even though too little ATP is made
    4. It turns NADH directly into glucose
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: the extra NADH slows the cycle down instead.
    2. Wrong: aerobic respiration prefers a one-to-one ratio, so upsetting it matters.
    3. Right: high NADH is a false "full tank" signal to the cell.
    4. Wrong: the chapter says glucose use and the making of new sugars both slow down.

    Each step of ethanol breakdown (ADH, then ALDH) turns an NAD+ into NADH. The resulting excess "signals the cell that it has plenty of energy," which inhibits the citric acid cycle.

  15. Which hypothesis about alcohol and ATP production does the chapter best support?

    1. ATP production falls, because the liver breaks down ethanol before glucose and extra NADH slows aerobic respiration
    2. ATP production rises, because ethanol is a better fuel than glucose
    3. ATP production is unchanged, because the liver handles ethanol and glucose at the same time
    4. ATP production falls only because alcohol makes people sleep more
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: glucose conversion is put on hold and the electron transport chain can't make enough ATP.
    2. Wrong: the chapter says glucose is the preferred source of energy, and ethanol blocks its use.
    3. Wrong: the liver prioritizes ethanol, putting glucose on hold.
    4. Wrong: alcohol does affect sleep, but the chapter traces the ATP drop to the metabolic pathway itself.

    Two mechanisms combine: the liver puts glucose on hold to process ethanol, and the NADH build-up inhibits the citric acid cycle and glycolysis, so the final stage "can't produce sufficient ATP for the body."

  16. A classmate argues: "Ethanol breakdown makes extra NADH, and the electron transport chain uses NADH to make ATP, so drinking should boost ATP." How would you evaluate this?

    1. It is correct, because more NADH always means more ATP
    2. It is correct, because ADH makes ATP directly
    3. It is wrong, because ethanol breakdown actually uses up NADH
    4. It is wrong, because the excess NADH falsely signals plenty of energy, inhibiting the cycle so less ATP is made
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: the chapter shows the opposite: too much NADH throws off the one-to-one ratio and slows respiration.
    2. Wrong: ADH turns ethanol into acetaldehyde and NAD+ into NADH; the chapter doesn't say it makes ATP.
    3. Wrong: ethanol breakdown turns NAD+ into NADH, so NADH goes up.
    4. Right: the false signal inhibits the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain falls short.

    The argument sounds logical but ignores regulation. The chapter says high NADH inhibits the citric acid cycle "even though in reality, there isn't enough ATP produced."

  17. According to the chapter, how does heavy drinking lead to fat building up in the liver?

    1. Acetaldehyde is turned directly into fat
    2. Alcohol speeds up fat breakdown, which leaves fatty waste behind
    3. The liver stops breaking down fat while it handles ethanol, and unused acetyl CoA is made into fatty acids
    4. Ghrelin from the stomach tells the liver to store fat
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: acetaldehyde becomes acetic acid and then CO2 and H2O.
    2. Wrong: the liver prioritizes ethanol, so fat breakdown halts rather than speeds up.
    3. Right: both routes add fat, which can lead to alcohol-induced liver disease.
    4. Wrong: ghrelin is a hunger hormone and is not part of this pathway.

    When the citric acid cycle slows, acetyl CoA isn't used, and the liver uses it for fatty acid synthesis. As fat builds up, the liver becomes inflamed, cells die and scar tissue replaces healthy tissue.

    4. The SCN stops sending signals to the liver altogether
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Wrong: the chapter says the liver processes about one drink per hour; it doesn't say this speeds up.
    2. Right: an out-of-phase clock makes the liver susceptible to injury, and declining function offsets the clock further.
    3. Wrong: it is the reverse: healthy liver tissue is replaced with scar tissue.
    4. Wrong: the chapter blames the liver's own clock falling out of phase, not a total loss of SCN signals.

  19. How can alcohol's damage to the liver's clock end up affecting the gut?

    1. A weak liver clock weakens other organs; the gut lining can be destroyed and let toxins in ("leaky gut")
    2. The liver sends acetaldehyde to the stomach, which stops ghrelin
    3. The gut is not affected; only the liver is harmed
    4. Alcohol strengthens the gut lining to protect the liver
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: this is the chapter's account of "leaky gut," which can cause digestive issues, fatigue and joint discomfort.
    2. Wrong: the chapter does not describe this pathway.
    3. Wrong: the chapter says a weak liver clock weakens other organs as well.
    4. Wrong: the gut lining is damaged, not strengthened.

    Unstable metabolism spreads: "fat accumulation can harm the liver, which can further break down the gut lining" (Breus, 2018).

  20. Why can blood sugar drop to dangerous levels after drinking?

    1. Alcohol makes the pancreas release extra GLP-1

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 and LO2 · If you moved all your meals two hours later but kept your sleep the same, what would change and what wouldn't? Name the zeitgebers involved.

After about a month you would feel hungry at the new meal times, because regular eating entrains peripheral clocks, partly through ghrelin spikes at usual meal times. The stomach's PER1 and PER2 work mostly independently of the SCN, so food timing is the zeitgeber here. Melatonin and cortisol, the master clock markers set by light and sleep/wake times, would stay mostly unchanged, as in the Wehrens study, where insulin shifted with the meals and they didn't.

LO3 · Explain how ghrelin, leptin and GLP-1 work together to control hunger, and where each comes from.

Ghrelin comes from PD/D1 cells in the stomach when it is empty or near a regular meal time. It is orexigenic and activates AgRP/NPY neurons in the arcuate nucleus. GLP-1 comes from L-cells in the ileum during digestion: it triggers insulin in the pancreas and slows stomach emptying, but it breaks down within minutes. Leptin comes from fat cells when fat stores are high and is the long-term signal: it activates POMC neurons, sending a "don't eat" signal, the near opposite of ghrelin.

LO4 and LO5 · Predict what happens to hunger in someone who undereats for a long time, then explain what rat feeding studies reveal about meal timing.

Leptin is made by fat tissue, so when fat shrinks, leptin plummets and the person becomes ravenously hungry, and if dangerously underfed the body goes into starvation mode. In rats, a strict feeding schedule produces food-anticipatory activity 2-4 hours before meals. It continued when food was withheld and after SCN lesions or light deprivation, so meal routine entrains a separate peripheral clock, the FEO, much like meal timing shifted human insulin without moving melatonin or cortisol.

LO6 and LO7 · Walk through the four stages of aerobic respiration, then describe how alcohol changes the daily rhythm of the liver's metabolism.

Glycolysis in the cytosol splits glucose into 2 pyruvate. Pyruvate oxidation in the mitochondria makes acetyl CoA. The citric acid cycle releases ATP, NADH, FADH2 and CO2, and the electron transport chain uses the NADH and FADH2 to make 32 ATP. Normally the liver turns energy creation up in active hours and down at rest. Alcohol raises NADH and lowers NAD+, which makes the citric acid cycle act as if energy is abundant, halting glucose breakdown and shifting the liver's internal clock.

LO8 · Present a hypothesis for how drinking alcohol affects ATP production, and back it up with the chapter's evidence.

Hypothesis: drinking lowers ATP production. The liver breaks down ethanol before glucose, so glucose conversion is put on hold. Each step of ethanol breakdown turns NAD+ into NADH, so NADH builds up past the one-to-one ratio respiration prefers. The cell reads this as plenty of energy and inhibits the citric acid cycle, glycolysis slows, and the electron transport chain can't make enough ATP, causing muscle fatigue and lethargy.

LO9 and LO10 · Explain how alcohol damages the liver, and how that damage can spread to other parts of the body.

With the citric acid cycle slowed, unused acetyl CoA is made into fatty acids, and the liver stops breaking down fat while it handles ethanol. Fat builds up and can cause alcohol-induced liver disease: the liver becomes inflamed, cells die and scar tissue replaces healthy tissue. Its out-of-phase clock makes it susceptible to injury and even cancer. A weak liver clock weakens other organs: the gut lining can be destroyed and let toxins in, causing "leaky gut" with digestive issues, fatigue and joint discomfort. Blood sugar can also drop dangerously.
