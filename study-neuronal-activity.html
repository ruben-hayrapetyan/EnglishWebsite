The chapter in 16 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order. Figures are the chapter's own.

1 · What is being measured

One wire, two signals

  • A thin electrode in the brain records voltage outside the cells: extracellular recording.
  • The result is a noisy, wave-like line that contains both spikes and local field potentials (LFPs).
  • Telling them apart, and telling both apart from voltage measured inside a cell, is the first step.

1 · What is being measured

Anatomy of a spike

  • A spike is an action potential: a rapid voltage change across the membrane that neurons use to communicate.
  • Rest is about −70 mV. Stimuli push it up to a threshold near −55 mV.
  • Voltage-gated sodium channels open: depolarization up to about +30 mV.
  • Sodium channels inactivate, potassium rushes out: repolarization.
  • All or nothing: the neuron either reaches threshold and fires, or doesn't. Exact values vary across neurons.

1 · What is being measured

A voice versus the room

  • Each neuron connects to roughly 1,000 to 10,000 others through synapses.
  • An LFP reflects the combined transmembrane currents of many neurons near the electrode, especially synaptic currents. It is smooth and changes gradually.
  • Scale is the main difference: a spike is one neuron, an LFP is a group. Neither is the whole picture.
  • Like a party: you can follow one conversation, or hear the volume of the room, but not both at once.
  • Spikes run in the hundreds of hertz, LFPs much lower, but spike waveforms leak down and can pollute fast LFP activity.

2 · Neural activity is rhythmic

Five common bands

  • Groups of neurons go active and idle together, over and over. The LFP records this as an oscillation.
  • Delta (δ) 1–4 Hz · theta (θ) 4–8 Hz · alpha (α) 8–12 Hz · beta (β) 15–30 Hz · gamma (γ) 30–100 Hz.
  • The lines aren't universal: labs draw them differently, and species run the same rhythm at different speeds.
  • Olfactory gamma is 40–100 Hz in rats and mice, and about 20 Hz in insects (Kay, 2014).

2 · Neural activity is rhythmic

Frequency and period

  • Frequency and period are reciprocals: a 40 Hz rhythm completes a cycle every 25 ms; a 4 Hz rhythm takes 250 ms.
  • A spike lasts about a millisecond, so dozens fit inside one slow cycle. When a spike lands in the cycle can carry information, not just how many spikes there are.
  • Every rhythm here is ultradian: many cycles per day. Gamma completes roughly 3.5 million cycles in one circadian day.

2 · Neural activity is rhythmic

Do oscillations do anything?

  • A bridge vibrates under traffic, but that's not why it was built. Are brain rhythms just vibration?
  • Buzsáki & Draguhn (2004): oscillations span five orders of magnitude and are preserved across species, so they likely serve functions.
  • Proposed roles: bias which inputs reach a population, temporarily link neurons, support synaptic changes in learning.
  • Most claims are correlational. Olfactory gamma is stronger evidence: disrupting it impaired fine odor discrimination in insects; enhancing it improved discrimination in mice (Kay, 2014).

2 · Neural activity is rhythmic

Phase: when in the cycle

  • Some neurons fire at a preferred point in the LFP cycle rather than at random. That point is the phase.
  • A neuron firing near troughs and one firing near peaks can have identical firing rates yet contribute differently to the circuit.
  • The group rhythm shapes spike timing; individual neurons supply the spikes.

3 · Connection to slower body rhythms

Breathing reaches the brain

  • Neural activity synchronized to breathing appears across mammals, humans included (Tort et al., 2025).
  • Expected in smell areas, since odors arrive in pulses. Surprising: it extends into regions for emotion and higher cognition.
  • Airflow through the nose sends waves of sensory activity through the brain that can align neurons to the breath.
  • This is phase-amplitude coupling: the phase of a slow rhythm goes with changes in the amplitude of a faster one.

3 · Connection to slower body rhythms

Modulate is not generate

  • Breathing can modulate or entrain existing brain rhythms; it doesn't generate them. Brain oscillations continue without nasal airflow.
  • It supplies a shared reference signal distant regions can lock onto. Synchrony alone doesn't prove direct communication.
  • Rodent sniffing (about 4–12 Hz) overlaps theta. An 8 Hz hippocampal rhythm could be theta, respiratory coupling, or both; you have to record breathing to tell.
  • The general lesson: two rhythms with the same period are not necessarily the same rhythm. Slower rhythms can coordinate faster ones (see Circulation and the cardiac cycle).

4 · Getting a signal from a recording

Spike sorting in four steps

  1. Bandpass filter the recording, typically 300–3,000 Hz.
  2. Threshold: scan for moments the voltage crosses a set limit.
  3. Measure the shape of each detected waveform.
  4. Cluster similar shapes, treating each cluster as possibly one neuron (Rey et al., 2015).

Stop after step 2 and count every crossing, and you get multi-unit activity: how much the local population fires, not which cell did what.

4 · Getting a signal from a recording

Where sorting goes wrong

  • It assumes each neuron makes a consistent waveform, different from its neighbors' because of distance and orientation to the tip.
  • With many neurons close to the electrode, waveforms look too alike and accuracy drops.
  • Two spikes at the same instant overlap into a shape that matches neither neuron.
  • Rarely firing neurons get lost under busier neighbors, which matters for sparse cells in memory tasks (Rey et al., 2015).
  • Net effect: datasets can be biased toward neurons with distinctive, detectable waveforms.

4 · Getting a signal from a recording

Finding rhythms: the Fourier transform

Any complex repeating signal can be rebuilt from sine waves of different frequencies, amplitudes and phases.

X(f)=∫−∞∞x(t)e−i2πftdt

It asks one question: how much of the signal x(t) looks like a wave of frequency f? Ask it for every frequency and you get the power spectrum:

P(f)∝|X(f)|2

A tall peak at 8 Hz means strong theta; one at 60 Hz means gamma.

4 · Getting a signal from a recording

Which frequencies, but not when

  • One spectrum for a whole recording summarizes frequency content without showing when each rhythm happened. (It doesn't assume the rhythm is steady.)
  • Gamma comes in short bursts; theta waxes and wanes. Averaged over time, a brief strong burst and a weak continuous rhythm can look alike.
  • The wavelet transform swaps the endless sine wave for a short one that can be moved and stretched, giving frequency at a given moment:
W(a,b)=1a∫−∞∞x(t)ψ∗(t−ba)dt

4 · Getting a signal from a recording

Limits of any recording

  • Sampled fs times a second, the highest recoverable frequency is the Nyquist frequency:
fNyquist=fs2
  • Without filtering first, faster frequencies fold back as false slow ones: aliasing. Spike-sorting recordings often sample above 20,000 Hz with anti-aliasing filters.
  • An LFP reflects population currents but not which cells produced them. Volume conduction means some of it comes from beyond the tip.
  • LFP amplitude depends on how sources are arranged: a weak, well-aligned source can beat a strong, disorganized one (Herreras, 2016; Pesaran et al., 2018). Recovering the activity needs a model of how the signal was made (Einevoll et al., 2013).

5 · From biological to artificial

An equation with no time in it

A basic artificial neural network unit weights its inputs, sums them and passes the total through a function:

y=φ(∑iwixi+b)

There is no spike time t. The output is a number, not a timed event, so phase and spike timing are not represented. A leaky integrate-and-fire model puts time back:

τmdVdt=−(V(t)−Vrest)+RmI(t)

Voltage builds with input current and leaks back toward rest; at threshold it spikes and resets.

5 · From biological to artificial

Learning from spike timing

  • Spiking neural networks pass timed pulses instead of continuous values.
  • In one form of STDP, the weight change depends on the interval Δt=tpost−tpre and its sign.
  • Neuron A fires just before B: the connection strengthens. Just after: it weakens. Longer intervals mean smaller changes. Real plasticity depends on circuit and conditions.
  • Imam & Cleland (2020) built an olfactory-bulb-style network on Intel's Loihi neuromorphic chip, applying STDP over sequential gamma-frequency packets.
  • The tested model learned to tell odors apart after limited exposure, even with interference from other odors.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Action potential (spike)
A rapid reversal of the membrane potential that propagates along an axon to transmit information between neurons.
Membrane potential
The difference in voltage between the inside and outside of a cell membrane.
Axon (and hillock)
The axon is the long tail of the neuron that conducts electrical impulses away from itself. The axon hillock is the specialized region of the cell body that connects to the axon where the action potentials are created.
Refractory period
The short time after a neuron fires (action potential) where it cannot fire again in succession, unless a much stronger stimulus is provided.
Extracellular recording
A method of measuring electrical activity from outside the cell membrane (using electrodes).
Intracellular recording
A method of measuring electrical activity in just a single cell (with a microelectrode).
Local field potential (LFP)
The recorded extracellular electrical signal that reflects the synaptic activity of a local population of cells.
Oscillation
Repeating and rhythmic fluctuations in electrical activity across a group of neurons.
Frequency
The number of cycles (waves) an oscillation completes in a second (measured in hertz).
Phase
The position of a wave at a specific point with respect to a complete cycle.
Power spectral density
A mathematical measure that shows how the energy of a signal is distributed across different frequencies.
Bandpass filter
A signal processing tool that blocks frequencies outside a certain range, allowing to single out certain wavelengths.
Phase-amplitude coupling
A special type of cross-frequency coupling of a brain signal interaction where the strength (amplitude) of a fast brain wave is controlled by the cycle (phase) of a slow brain wave.
Cross-frequency coupling
An interaction between different frequency bands where neural oscillations interact with each other to process information.
Entrainment
The interaction where an internal brain rhythm is affected by external periodic rhythms (such as sensory stimulus or behavioral cycles).
Spike sorting
A data processing step used to group extracellular signals to individual neurons.
Thresholding
A processing technique used to separate real neural signals from noise using a specific voltage limit.
Multi-unit activity (MUA)
The combined activity of several neurons that are recorded by a single electrode.
Signal-to-noise ratio
A measure that compares the meaningful neural parts of a signal to background noise.
Fourier transform
A mathematical tool that is used to break down complex signals into its individual sine wave frequency components, revealing which rhythms are present and how strong they are.
Wavelet transform
A similar tool to the Fourier transform that breaks down a signal into localized wavelets, allowing analysis of how frequency components change over time.
Nyquist frequency
The highest frequency that can be captured without distortions (half the sampling rate).
Aliasing
Distortions and "ghost frequencies" that occur when not abiding by the Nyquist frequency threshold.
Volume conduction
The spread of electrical current through brain tissue, allowing an electrode to record signals generated away from its tip.
Artificial neural network (ANN)
A computer program modeled after biological brains, where continuous values are created by summing weighted inputs rather than discrete spikes.
Synaptic weights
In an artificial neural network, these are the values that determine how strong the connection between two nodes are (how much they influence one another).
Integrate-and-fire model
A model of a neuron that adds up incoming electrical inputs over time until a threshold is reached and then "fires."
Spiking neural network (SNN)
An artificial neural network that uses timed pulses (spikes) instead of continuous values to communicate.
Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)
An algorithm used to update the connection model weights of an ANN where the change in a synaptic connection depends on the interval between a presynaptic and postsynaptic spike (and which came first).
Neuromorphic computing
An approach to computer engineering where systems are designed to mimic natural biological processes and structures of the nervous system.
Neuromorphic hardware
Specially designed circuits designed to use brain-like structures to execute neural networks.
Memristor
An electronic component that mimics biological synapses by "remembering" the amount of charge that previously flowed through it.

Check yourself

Seventeen questions covering all five objectives. Answer without looking back, say how sure you are, and read every explanation, including the ones you get right.

  1. Which description fits a local field potential (LFP)?

    1. The combined transmembrane currents of many neurons near the electrode, especially synaptic currents
    2. The voltage change across one neuron's membrane when it fires
    3. Electrical noise from the electrode that must be filtered out before analysis
    4. The voltage at the axon hillock just before threshold
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    An LFP reflects the combined transmembrane currents of many neurons, especially synaptic currents, recorded near an electrode (Buzsáki et al., 2012). Unlike a spike, it is smooth and changes gradually, and its amplitude also depends on the geometry of those currents.

  2. A neuron at rest sits near −70 mV. What happens once stimuli push it to about −55 mV?

    1. Voltage-gated sodium channels open and the cell depolarizes sharply, to about +30 mV
    2. Potassium channels open and the voltage drops below rest
    3. The neuron fires a spike whose size depends on how far past threshold it went
    4. Nothing yet; it needs to reach 0 mV before it fires
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    At threshold (around −55 mV) voltage-gated sodium channels open, causing depolarization up to about +30 mV. Then sodium channels inactivate and potassium channels open, bringing the voltage back down (repolarization). The neuron either reaches threshold and fires or stays below and doesn't; exact values vary across neurons.

  3. Which statement best captures the main difference between spikes and LFPs?

    1. Scale: a spike tells you what one neuron is doing; an LFP tells you what a group is doing together
    2. They need different electrodes, so they're never recorded together
    3. LFPs are faster than spikes
    4. LFPs are more accurate than spikes, so spikes are rarely used
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Like a party: you can follow one person's conversation (a spike) or hear the volume of the room (an LFP), but tuning into one means losing the other. Both are picked up by the same electrode.

  4. A rhythm cycles 6 times per second. Which band is it in?

    1. Delta
    2. Theta
    3. Alpha
    4. Gamma
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Delta 1–4 Hz, theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–12 Hz, beta 15–30 Hz, gamma 30–100 Hz. Labs draw these lines in slightly different places, and different animals run the same functional rhythm at different speeds.

  5. A gamma rhythm runs at 40 Hz. How many milliseconds does one cycle take?

    Show the answer

    Answer: 25 ms

    Frequency and period are reciprocals: 1 ÷ 40 Hz = 0.025 s = 25 ms. By the same rule a 4 Hz rhythm takes 250 ms per cycle. A spike lasts about 1 ms, so dozens can fit in one slow cycle.

  6. Neuron A tends to fire near the peaks of an LFP cycle; neuron B near the troughs. Both fire at the same average rate. What follows?

    1. They are doing the same thing, since only firing rate carries information
    2. They may contribute differently to circuit activity, because they fire at different phases
    3. Neuron B is effectively silent, because troughs are when neurons rest
    4. Phase only matters if the rhythm is in the gamma band
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Phase is the position of a wave relative to one complete cycle. Some neurons prefer a particular phase. Two neurons with identical rates can still contribute differently depending on when in the cycle they fire: the group rhythm shapes spike timing, while individual neurons supply the spikes.

  7. Why did Buzsáki and Draguhn (2004) argue that brain oscillations are more than a byproduct, like a bridge vibrating under traffic?

    1. They span five orders of magnitude in frequency and are preserved across species
    2. They are all generated by breathing
    3. Every function of every rhythm has been proven by causal experiments
    4. They only appear in the human brain
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    They also proposed that oscillations can bias which inputs reach a population, temporarily link neurons, and support synaptic changes in learning. Support varies by circuit; olfactory gamma gives some of the strongest evidence (Kay, 2014).

  8. In breathing-related phase-amplitude coupling, what is linked to what?

    1. The phase of the slow breathing cycle goes with changes in the amplitude of a faster brain rhythm
    2. The amplitude of the breathing cycle sets the phase of gamma
    3. Breathing and gamma run at the same frequency
    4. Breathing generates the fast rhythm from scratch
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Airflow through the nose sends waves of sensory activity through the brain, which can align neurons with the breathing cycle and modulate faster oscillations (Tort et al., 2025). In Figure A4, gamma bursts are largest at one point in each breath.

  9. A rat is sniffing, and you record an 8 Hz rhythm in its hippocampus. What is the safest conclusion?

    1. It is hippocampal theta
    2. It is respiratory coupling, because sniffing runs at 4–12 Hz
    3. It could be theta, respiratory coupling or both; you'd need to record breathing to separate them
    4. It proves the hippocampus and the nose are communicating directly
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Rodent sniffing (about 4–12 Hz) overlaps theta. The two can occur at similar frequencies without being continuously synchronized (Kay, 2014). This is a recurring problem across chronobiology: two rhythms with the same period are not necessarily the same rhythm.

  10. If nasal airflow stops, what happens to brain oscillations, according to the chapter?

    1. They stop, because breathing drives them
    2. They continue; breathing modulates or entrains existing rhythms but doesn't generate them all
    3. They all shift into the delta band
    4. Only the olfactory rhythms continue
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Breathing supplies a shared reference signal that distant regions can lock onto, which can help coordinate activity. The broader principle: slower rhythms can coordinate faster ones, across many timescales.

  11. What is the order of the four spike sorting steps?

    1. Cluster, threshold, filter, measure shape
    2. Bandpass filter, threshold, measure waveform shape, cluster
    3. Threshold, bandpass filter, cluster, measure waveform shape
    4. Measure waveform shape, bandpass filter, threshold, cluster
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Bandpass filter (typically 300–3,000 Hz), find threshold crossings, measure each waveform's shape, then cluster similar shapes as possibly coming from one neuron (Rey et al., 2015).

  12. A researcher stops after thresholding and simply counts every crossing, without assigning any to a cell. What do they have?

    1. Sorted single-unit activity
    2. Multi-unit activity
    3. A local field potential
    4. A power spectrum
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Multi-unit activity records how much the local population is firing, without knowing which specific cells are responsible.

  13. Which neurons is spike sorting most likely to miss?

    1. Neurons with distinctive waveforms
    2. Neurons that fire rarely, next to neighbors that fire often
    3. Neurons that fire very often
    4. Neurons that fire at the peak of the LFP
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Rarely firing neurons are easily lost under neighbors that fire more often, which matters for sparse cells such as some involved in memory tasks (Rey et al., 2015). Accuracy also drops when many neurons sit close to the electrode, and overlapping spikes can match neither neuron.

  14. A whole-recording power spectrum shows a big theta peak and only a tiny bump at 60 Hz. Why might a wavelet (time-frequency) analysis tell you more?

    1. The Fourier transform assumes rhythms never change, so it's wrong
    2. It shows when each frequency occurred, so brief 60 Hz bursts stand out instead of being averaged away
    3. Wavelets can recover frequencies above the Nyquist limit
    4. Wavelets remove spike contamination from the LFP
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    The wavelet transform swaps the infinite sine wave for a short wave that can be moved and stretched, giving information about a given frequency at a given moment. Figure A6 shows the 60 Hz bursts nearly vanishing in the whole-record spectrum but standing out clearly in the spectrogram.

  15. A signal is sampled 1,000 times per second. What is the highest frequency that can be recovered?

    Show the answer

    Answer: 500 Hz

    The Nyquist frequency is half the sampling rate: 1,000 ÷ 2 = 500 Hz. Without filtering first, anything faster folds back and shows up as a misleading lower frequency (aliasing). That's why spike-sorting recordings often sample above 20,000 Hz with anti-aliasing filters.

  16. In the basic artificial neuron y=φ(∑iwixi+b), what is missing that a spiking model has?

    1. Synaptic weights
    2. An offset
    3. An explicit spike time
    4. Inputs
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Nothing in the feed-forward equation says when a spike happened, so spike timing and phase aren't represented. A leaky integrate-and-fire model outputs a discrete spike at a specific time. (Other artificial networks can process time-dependent inputs.)

  17. Under a common STDP rule, neuron A fires a few milliseconds before neuron B, which it connects to. What happens to the A-to-B connection?

    1. It strengthens
    2. It weakens
    3. It stays the same, because only firing rate matters
    4. It strengthens more the longer A waits before B fires
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    The weight change depends on Δt = tpost − tpre and its sign. Pre just before post strengthens; pre just after post weakens; longer intervals mean smaller changes. Actual plasticity depends on the circuit and conditions.

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer. Explaining is harder than recognizing, which is why it sticks.

LO1 · A friend asks what the "wiggly line" from a brain electrode is. What do you tell them?

It's an extracellular recording: voltage measured outside cells. It mixes two things. Spikes are brief, sharp action potentials from individual nearby neurons. The LFP is a slower, smoother wave from the combined currents of many neurons, mostly synaptic. One is a single voice; the other is the volume of the room.

LO2 · Why could the timing of a spike matter, not just how many spikes there are?

A spike lasts about 1 ms, while slow cycles last tens to hundreds of ms, so many spikes fit in one cycle. Neurons can prefer a phase of the cycle. Two neurons with equal rates but different phases can contribute differently to the circuit, so in some circuits where a spike lands carries information.

LO3 · Explain, using breathing, how a slow body rhythm can shape a fast brain rhythm.

Airflow through the nose sends waves of sensory activity into the brain, which can align neurons with the breath. The strength of faster oscillations, like gamma, then rises and falls with the breathing phase: phase-amplitude coupling. Breathing modulates rhythms that already exist rather than creating them, and gives distant regions a shared reference to lock onto.

LO4 · Name one limitation of spike sorting and one of LFP analysis.

Spike sorting: rarely firing neurons get lost under busy neighbors, similar waveforms near the electrode get confused, and overlapping spikes match neither neuron, so datasets skew toward distinctive cells. LFP: it doesn't say which cells produced it, volume conduction brings in signal from beyond the tip, and amplitude depends on how sources are arranged, not just how active they are. A single spectrum also hides when rhythms happened, and slow sampling causes aliasing.

LO5 · How does a spiking network use time in a way a basic artificial network doesn't?

A basic artificial unit outputs a number with no time in it. A spiking network sends timed pulses, and rules like STDP change connection strength based on the precise order and interval of spikes: pre just before post strengthens, just after weakens. Imam and Cleland's olfactory circuit on Intel's Loihi chip used this over gamma-frequency packets to learn odors quickly, despite interference.
