The chapter in 17 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

What hair does for mammals

  • Hair serves thermoregulation, protection and sensory perception.
  • It can protect against physical injury and UV radiation, or provide camouflage against predators.
  • Specialized hairs like vibrissae let some animals sense their near environment through touch.
  • Humans have less body hair than other primates, but hair still serves important functions.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

A cycle that repeats

  • The hair follicle goes through continuous cycles of growth, degeneration, rest and regeneration.
  • Natarelli et al. describe four stages: anagen, catagen, telogen and exogen.
  • The cycle repeats again and again, gradually and in order.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

Anagen: the growth phase

  • Cells in the follicle divide and produce the hair shaft.
  • The follicle extends deeper into the skin as the hair gets longer.
  • How long a follicle stays in anagen helps determine how long the hair can grow.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

Catagen and telogen: stop, then rest

  • Catagen (transition): active production of the hair shaft ends and the lower portion of the follicle shrinks.
  • Telogen (resting): a fully formed hair stays in the follicle without growing.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

Exogen: the shedding phase

  • The old hair is released and falls out.
  • New hair begins to grow underneath it, and the cycle starts over.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

Humans: follicles out of step

  • Humans show asynchronized hair growth: follicles don't enter the four phases at the same time.
  • Each follicle cycles independently.
  • So the whole scalp never sheds at once, and hair coverage stays consistent over time.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

Other mammals: synchronized growth and molting

  • Some mammals show more synchronized hair growth: large portions of a coat may change seasonally.
  • When follicles enter shedding and replacement around the same time, the animal may molt.
  • Molting is a coordinated period of hair loss and coat replacement, often influenced by seasonal cues from the environment.

1 · Basics of hair growth and shedding

Chimpanzees and terminal length

  • Like human follicles, chimpanzee follicles are not synchronized, and chimpanzees don't normally go through a seasonal molt.
  • Their hair grows to its terminal length: the maximum length, set by how long anagen lasts.
  • Chimpanzees have a short anagen period, from a few weeks to months, so their hair stays shorter than human hair.

2 · Factors that impact the hair cycle

The cycle can be pushed off course

  • The hair cycle isn't a completely independent process.
  • Factors linked to changes in the cycle include inflammation, hormones, stress, nutritional deficiency, sleep quality and medications.
  • These internal, biological changes affect every stage of the cycle.

2 · Factors that impact the hair cycle

Stress: useful at first, harmful when repeated

  • Stress is a physiological response to a perceived or physical challenge.
  • Some stress supports survival: it affects cardiovascular activity, memory, energy, alertness and immune signalling.
  • Repeated stress may disrupt body maintenance, and can affect hair follicles through the neuroendocrine pathway by changing when they regenerate.

2 · Factors that impact the hair cycle

Cortisol as a stress marker

  • Stress is often associated with changes in cortisol, a steroid hormone made by the adrenal glands.
  • Because of this, cortisol is commonly used as a biomarker of physiological stress levels.

2 · Factors that impact the hair cycle

Testing stress with an animal model

  • An animal model in a controlled environment can show how stress affects the hair cycle.
  • Animal studies can give information relevant to humans while avoiding the ethical concerns of experimenting on people.
  • A well-designed experiment has an independent variable, a dependent variable and a control group.
  • Researchers change one factor and compare biological responses to see whether that change had an effect.

2 · Factors that impact the hair cycle

Design your own stress experiment

  • Test whether changes in the hair cycle follow stress level, without exposing animals to harmful stress.
  • State a question and a hypothesis: predict how lower or higher cortisol relates to hair growth and shedding.
  • Specify variables and groups: name the manipulated intervention, keep other factors constant, and explain group assignments.
  • Then evaluate causation.

3 · Potential treatment and intervention

Two ways to treat heavy shedding

  • Disrupted shedding can be addressed with nutritional support or medical therapies.
  • Follicles need enough nutrients and enough biological signalling to keep growing hair normally.
  • How well each approach works depends on the cause of the hair loss.

3 · Potential treatment and intervention

Nutritional support

  • Keeping up a diet that corrects deficiencies which may interfere with hair growth.
  • Iron, zinc and vitamin D support follicle development; deficiencies may increase shedding and reduce growth.
  • Excessive supplementation doesn't really improve hair growth and may even cause adverse side effects.

3 · Potential treatment and intervention

Medical therapies: minoxidil and finasteride

  • Medical therapies target specific biological mechanisms of hair loss.
  • Minoxidil increases blood flow and follicle activity, and promotes the move from telogen to anagen.
  • Finasteride inhibits 5-alpha-reductase, the enzyme that turns testosterone into DHT.
  • DHT causes follicular miniaturization (thinner hairs), so reducing it helps maintain hair growth.

3 · Potential treatment and intervention

Match the treatment to the cause

  • Neither approach is a universally effective intervention.
  • Nutritional support fits best when the patient has a nutrient deficiency.
  • Medical therapies fit best when a specific hormonal mechanism is involved.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Anagen phase
Growth phase of the hair cycle; the hair follicle rapidly divides, the hair shaft lengthens, and the follicle remains deeply embedded into the skin.
Catagen phase
Transition phase of hair cycle; the lower part of the hair follicle shrinks and separates from the blood supply.
Telogen phase
The resting phase of the hair cycle; the hair follicle is more inactive, and the fully formed hair remains in the follicle, without growing.
Exogen phase
Shedding phase of the hair cycle; the old hair is released and falls out, and new hair begins to grow underneath it.
Hair follicle niche
A microenvironment surrounding hair follicle stem cells, including extracellular matrix, blood vessels, nerves, signaling molecules, and other factors that regulate cell-stem regeneration and growth.
Mammals
Animals that have hair or fur at some point in their lives, which help with insulation, sensation, and protection.
Asynchronized
Hair follicles cycle independently, so all the different hairs are in different growth phases at the same time. Asynchronized growth is more typical and helps prevent large amounts of hair from shedding at once.
Synchronized hair growth
Groups of hair follicles that enter the same growth/shedding cycle at the same time (synchronously). Common in many animals that go through seasonal coat changes.
Molting
A seasonal periodic shedding and replacement of hair or fur. Molting can result in a synchronized change in hair growth cycle for mammals.
Terminal length
Biologically controlled maximum length a hair can reach, determined by how long the follicle remains in anagen.
Cortisol
A steroid hormone produced by adrenal glands. Regulates the body’s response to stress and immune activity. Cortisol is usually used as an indicator for stress, as its level often increases during physical or psychological stress.
Animal model
A non-human animal that is used in scientific research to study biological processes, treatments, responses, etc. that may be relevant to humans.
Independent variable
The factor that the researcher changes or manipulates in an experiment.
Dependent variable
The factor that is measured or observed to determine the effect of the independent variable.
Control group
A group that does not receive the experimental treatment and receives standard conditions. Provides a baseline to compare with the experimental group.
HPA axis
A neuroendocrine system involving the hypothalamus and adrenal glands that regulates the body’s physiological response to stress, including cortisol release.
DHT
A hormone produced from testosterone by the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase. DHT can contribute to hair follicle miniaturization and hair loss.
Follicular miniaturization
A process in which hair follicles progressively shrink and produce thinner hairs, often involved with the effects of DHT in hair loss.
Nutritional support
Use of diet and nutrients to help maintain health, support body functions, and correct nutritional deficiencies.
Medical therapy
Treatment used to improve or cure a medical condition, which involves medication, procedures, or interventions.

  1. A follicle has just stopped producing the hair shaft, and its lower portion is shrinking. Which phase is it in?

    1. Telogen
    2. Catagen
    3. Exogen
    4. Anagen
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Telogen comes after this: it's the resting phase, when a fully formed hair just sits in the follicle. The shrinking happens before that.
    2. Right: catagen is the transition phase, when hair shaft production ends and the lower follicle shrinks.
    3. Exogen is when the old hair is released and falls out. Nothing has been shed yet here.
    4. Anagen is the opposite: the follicle is producing the shaft and extending deeper into the skin.

    The chapter describes catagen as the transition stage between growth and rest: active production of the hair shaft ends and the lower portion of the follicle shrinks (Natarelli et al., 2023).

  2. Two people have follicles that produce hair at the same rate, but one person's hair can grow much longer. Which difference best explains this?

    1. Their follicles stay in telogen for longer
    2. Their hairs are shed more often in exogen
    3. Their follicles stay in anagen for longer
    4. Their follicles skip catagen
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. In telogen the hair rests without growing, so a longer rest adds no length.
    2. Shedding more often would end each hair's life sooner, not make it longer.
    3. Right: hair only lengthens in anagen, so the time a follicle spends there sets how long the hair can grow.
    4. The chapter describes all four phases happening in order every cycle; it never mentions skipping one, and catagen is not where growth happens.

    The chapter stresses that anagen is essential because how long a follicle stays in it determines how long hair can grow. This is the same idea as terminal length.

  3. A hair is fully formed and still anchored in its follicle, but it isn't getting any longer and hasn't been released. Which phase is this?

    2. Exogen
    3. Catagen
    4. Telogen
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. In anagen the hair is actively getting longer. This hair isn't growing.
    2. In exogen the old hair is released and falls out. This one hasn't been released.
    3. Catagen is the transition where shaft production stops and the follicle shrinks; the hair is not yet simply resting.
    4. Right: telogen is the resting phase, when a fully formed hair remains in the follicle without growing.

    Telogen is the resting phase: the follicle is more inactive and the fully formed hair stays put without growing, until exogen releases it.

  4. Which order matches one full turn of the hair cycle, starting with growth?

    1. Anagen, catagen, telogen, exogen
    2. Anagen, telogen, catagen, exogen
    3. Exogen, catagen, anagen, telogen
    4. Anagen, exogen, catagen, telogen
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: growth, then transition, then rest, then shedding, and the cycle repeats.
    2. This puts rest before transition. The follicle has to stop production and shrink (catagen) before the hair rests.
    3. Shedding doesn't lead into transition; after exogen, new hair grows, which is anagen.
    4. This sheds the hair straight after growth. The hair must stop growing and rest before it is released.

    The chapter lists the four stages in order: anagen (growth), catagen (transition), telogen (rest) and exogen (shedding), with new hair starting to grow under the old one as it falls out.

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 · Walk a single hair follicle through one full cycle, saying what happens in each phase.

It starts in anagen, the growth phase: cells in the follicle divide and make the hair shaft, and the follicle reaches deeper into the skin as the hair lengthens. How long it stays here sets how long the hair can grow. In catagen, the transition, shaft production stops and the lower follicle shrinks. In telogen, the resting phase, the fully formed hair just sits in the follicle without growing. In exogen, the shedding phase, the old hair is released and falls out while new hair starts growing underneath, and the cycle begins again.

LO2 · How are human, chimpanzee and molting-mammal hair cycles alike, and how are they different?

All of them go through the same phases. Humans and chimpanzees both have asynchronized follicles that cycle independently, so they never shed everything at once and don't normally molt. Many other mammals have more synchronized follicles, so large parts of the coat shed and get replaced together in a molt, often triggered by seasonal cues. Chimpanzees differ from humans in anagen length: theirs lasts only weeks to months, so their hair reaches a shorter terminal length.

LO3 · Outline an experiment to test whether stress disrupts the hair cycle, without exposing animals to harmful stress.

Use an animal model in a controlled environment. My question is whether higher stress changes hair growth and shedding, and I'd predict that higher cortisol goes with more disruption. The independent variable is a mild, non-harmful change I make for one group; the control group gets standard conditions as a baseline, and everything else stays constant. I'd measure cortisol as the stress biomarker, and hair growth and shedding as the dependent variable. To judge causation, I'd compare the groups and check that the only difference between them was the intervention.

LO4 · Is medical therapy better than nutritional support for hair cycle disruptions? Defend your answer with one scenario for each.

Neither is better in general: the right one depends on the cause. If someone low in iron, zinc or vitamin D is shedding more, nutritional support fits, because correcting the deficiency can restore healthy follicle activity, and no drug is needed (though extra supplements beyond that don't help and may cause side effects). If someone's follicles are miniaturizing because of DHT, medical therapy fits: finasteride blocks the enzyme that makes DHT, which diet can't do. Minoxidil is another targeted option that moves follicles from telogen into anagen.
