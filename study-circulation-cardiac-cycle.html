The chapter in 18 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · How the heart pumps blood

Four chambers, two trips

  • The heart has four hollow chambers: the right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle.
  • The right side takes deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs, where carbon dioxide is removed and the blood is oxygenated again.
  • The left side takes oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the rest of the body.

1 · How the heart pumps blood

Valves keep blood moving one way

  • Four valves prevent backflow: tricuspid (right atrium to right ventricle), pulmonary (to the pulmonary artery), mitral (left atrium to left ventricle) and aortic (to the aorta).
  • The valves are mostly controlled by pressure differences between the chambers.
  • The path: venae cavae, right atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary artery, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, left ventricle, aorta.

1 · How the heart pumps blood

A self-generating electrical system

  • The sinoatrial (SA) node, in the upper wall of the right atrium, is the primary pacemaker, firing 60-100 times per minute.
  • The atrioventricular (AV) node delays the signal by 0.1 seconds so the ventricles can fill before they contract.
  • The Bundle of His and Purkinje fibers then spread the impulse quickly through the ventricles for a coordinated contraction.

1 · How the heart pumps blood

Heart rate keeps a daily clock

  • Heart rate is highest during the day and lowest at night; at night the gap between beats is longer and conduction through the AV node and ventricles is slower.
  • On an unnatural non-24-hour schedule, heart rate still followed a roughly 24-hour rhythm, but was also lower during actual sleep: a mix of sleep and an internal clock.
  • The master clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), works with extrinsic factors such as temperature, time of day, light exposure and altitude.

2 · Sympathetic vs parasympathetic control

Two controls on one pacemaker

  • The sympathetic nervous system speeds the heartbeat up; the parasympathetic nervous system slows it down.
  • Both work through chemical signalling on the SA node, the heart's natural pacemaker.
  • Parasympathetic signals travel from the medulla through the vagus nerve using acetylcholine (ACh); sympathetic signals release norepinephrine (NE).

2 · Sympathetic vs parasympathetic control

Fight or flight, rest and relaxation

  • Sympathetic: in the thoracic spinal nerves, a group of 12 nerves; it puts the heart into a flight-or-fight response when muscles and organs need more oxygen and glucose.
  • Parasympathetic: mainly in the brainstem, with the vagus nerve as the main pathway.
  • The parasympathetic system is tied to rest and relaxation, and conserves energy when high output is not needed.

3 · Heart function with increased endurance training

Training makes each beat count

  • Endurance training repeatedly works the heart below its maximal heart rate, as in running, cycling and swimming; repeated sessions over weeks make the heart adapt.
  • The chambers, particularly the left ventricle, can enlarge and fill with more blood, so each beat ejects more: a larger stroke volume.
  • Training also shifts the resting balance toward parasympathetic influence. Together these lower the resting heart rate.

3 · Heart function with increased endurance training

Exercise can tell the heart the time

  • Heart rate and blood pressure rhythms are influenced by zeitgebers, outside cues that set rhythms through entrainment. Light is the best known; physical activity is a nonphotic zeitgeber.
  • Exercising at the same time each day may lead the cardiovascular system to anticipate it, raising heart rate and sympathetic activity slightly in advance.
  • Above the Arctic Circle, evening exercise in a dark winter delayed the peak of the heart rate rhythm compared with morning exercise and a control.

4 · Heart long-term health impacts

Aging lowers the ceiling

  • VO2max drops by about half between ages 20 and 80, because cardiac output and oxygen taken out of the blood each fall by about 25%.
  • Stroke volume stays roughly the same, so the fall in cardiac output is mostly a fall in maximum heart rate. Resting heart rate generally doesn't change.
  • The SA node ages: fat can build up around it, and fewer than 10% of its cells remain by age 75.
  • Longer electrical signals, thicker walls, less response to adrenaline and slower relaxing mean the older heart can't compensate at maximum effort.

4 · Heart long-term health impacts

Obesity adds strain

  • Obesity means extra blood in the body, so the heart pumps more every minute and works harder even at rest. It also raises blood pressure.
  • Pumping more blood under higher pressure thickens the heart's walls, leaving less space to fill; fat in the heart can cause stiff scar tissue.
  • Heart failure risk rises on average by 5% in men and 7% in women for every 1-unit BMI increase.

4 · Heart long-term health impacts

Shift work and circadian misalignment

  • When the SCN's clock doesn't match sleep and daylight timing, the heart clock can become less healthy; shift work is linked to higher rates of cardiovascular disease.
  • In day workers put on night shifts, waking blood pressure rose, the healthy drop in systolic blood pressure during sleep was dampened, and waking parasympathetic activity fell.
  • Regular shift workers showed raised blood pressure too, so they don't adapt; the internal clock, which uses slow-changing melatonin and cortisol, may not have time to adjust.

4 · Heart long-term health impacts

Smoking lowers heart rate variability

  • The heart's rhythm depends on a balance between sympathetic (SNS) and parasympathetic (PNS) activity.
  • Heart rate variability (HRV) shows that balance. The PNS acts quicker, so its activity gives higher HRV; low HRV means a less healthy, less adaptable heart.
  • Smoking raises sympathetic and lowers parasympathetic activity, lowering HRV. Nicotine is thought to be the main cause, and may make the heart more vulnerable to arrhythmias.

5 · Rhythm disruption

When the SA node fails

  • Sinus bradycardia: the SA node fires too slowly, often from a more active parasympathetic system. It can be normal in athletes.
  • Sinus node dysfunction, from aging or lost blood supply, has three types: sinus pause (fires late), sinus arrest (doesn't fire) and SA exit block (fires, but the signal can't leave the node).
  • Slow or skipped beats mean less blood reaches the brain, so patients can feel dizzy or faint; they usually need an artificial pacemaker.

5 · Rhythm disruption

Blocks at the AV node

  • First-degree AV block: every signal gets through, but abnormally delayed.
  • Second-degree: some beats are dropped, after a gradually lengthening delay (Mobitz I) or at random (Mobitz II). First- and second-degree blocks are often without symptoms.
  • Third-degree (complete): no signals get through, so the ventricles rely on a much slower, unreliable backup rhythm, which can cause bradycardia, low blood pressure and heart failure.

5 · Rhythm disruption

Too slow, too fast

  • Bradyarrhythmias beat slower than 60 beats per minute; tachyarrhythmias faster than 100.
  • Tachyarrhythmias are supraventricular if they start above the AV node, ventricular if below. Atrial fibrillation, the most common, makes the atria quiver instead of squeezing.
  • Ventricular arrhythmias are usually more dangerous because the ventricles drive blood to the body; ventricular tachycardia is a large cause of sudden cardiac death in the US.

5 · Rhythm disruption

Heart failure and ejection fraction

  • Heart failure: the heart can't fill with or push out blood properly or fast enough. The leading cause is ischemic heart disease.
  • It is classified by ejection fraction: reduced (40% or less), mildly reduced (41-49%) or preserved (50% or more).
  • Reduced: damaged cells can't squeeze hard, so blood backs up into the lungs. Preserved: the ventricles are too stiff to relax and fill.

6 · Supporting heart rhythms artificially

Pacemakers and defibrillators

  • A pacemaker treats a heart beating too slowly from sinus node dysfunction or AV block. It has a pulse generator, leads threaded in through a vein, and sensing circuitry.
  • Single-chamber pacemakers are the most common; dual-chamber ones coordinate atria and ventricles for a more natural rhythm.
  • For ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation, an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) watches for dangerous rhythms and delivers high-energy shocks.

6 · Supporting heart rhythms artificially

Pumping for a weak heart

  • Mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices help a heart too weak to pump; temporary ones last hours to weeks, permanent ones months or years.
  • VA-ECMO (temporary) draws blood from the right atrium, oxygenates it outside the body and returns it to the aorta, with risks such as vessel damage and clots.
  • An LVAD such as the HeartMate 3 pumps blood from the tip of the left ventricle into the aorta, long term or until a transplant, with 2-year survival of 79% to 83.4%.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Sinoatrial (SA) node
A small collection of electrical cells in the upper wall of the right atrium that act as the primary pacemaker of the heart, generating electrical impulses at a rate of 60-100 times per minute.
Atrioventricular (AV) node
Receives the signal from the SA node and briefly delays its passage by 0.1 seconds until the ventricles can fully fill with blood before they contract.
Bundle of His
A collection of specialized heart muscle cells that transmits the impulse to the ventricles.
Purkinje fibers
Receive impulses from the bundle branches and deliver them quickly throughout the ventricles, which ensures coordinated contraction of the ventricles.
Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)
The body’s master circadian clock, in the hypothalamus, which tries to maintain the roughly 24-hour cycle with heart rate.
Sympathetic nervous system
Speeds up the heartbeat; it causes the heart to go into a flight-or-fight response, stimulating the SA node to increase heart rate.
Parasympathetic nervous system
Slows down the heart, mainly through the vagus nerve; it is tied to rest and relaxation and conserves energy when high output is not needed.
Endurance (aerobic) training
The process of repeatedly subjecting the heart to exercise at levels lower than the person’s maximal heart rate, such as running, cycling and swimming.
Stroke volume
The volume of blood ejected by each contraction of the heart.
Zeitgeber (and entrainment)
An outside cue that allows the outside world to create rhythms, a process called entrainment. Light is the best-known zeitgeber; physical activity is a nonphotic zeitgeber.
VO2max
The maximum oxygen used by the body’s cells per minute during intensive exercise.
Cardiac output
The amount of blood pumped per minute, equivalent to the heart rate multiplied by the amount of blood pushed out with each contraction.
Heart rate variability (HRV)
The variation in time between consecutive heartbeats, which shows how activated one nervous system is compared to the other.
Sinus bradycardia
When the SA node, the primary pacemaker of the heart, fires too slowly.
AV block
When the electrical signal that usually passes through the AV node is delayed or stopped.
Arrhythmia
When the heart’s beating pattern is abnormal; divided according to whether it is beating slower or faster than normal.
Atrial fibrillation
The most common arrhythmia, a type of supraventricular arrhythmia, when many small electrical signals loop in circles across the atria instead of going through one organized path.
Ejection fraction
The percentage of blood the heart holds that it actually pushes out each beat.
Implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD)
A device that helps prevent sudden cardiac death by looking for dangerous rhythms and delivering high-energy shocks to “restart” the heart and restore normal rhythms.
Left ventricular assist device (LVAD)
A device for long-term support that takes blood from the tip of the left ventricle and pumps it into the aorta.

  1. Blood has just picked up oxygen in the lungs. Which route does it take next?

    1. Right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve
    2. Pulmonary artery, right atrium, right ventricle, aorta
    3. Pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve, aorta
    4. Pulmonary veins, left atrium, tricuspid valve, left ventricle, pulmonary valve
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The right side handles deoxygenated blood coming back from the body, not oxygenated blood from the lungs.
    2. The pulmonary artery carries blood from the heart to the lungs, not from the lungs back to the heart.
    3. Right: oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left side, which pumps it out through the aorta.
    4. The chambers are right, but the valves are not: the tricuspid and pulmonary valves are on the right side.

    The chapter traces the full loop: venae cavae, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary valve, pulmonary artery, lungs, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aortic valve and aorta.

  2. Why does the AV node hold the electrical signal back for about 0.1 seconds?

    1. To give the SA node time to generate its next impulse
    2. So the ventricles can fully fill with blood before they contract
    3. To slow the heart down at night while we sleep
    4. So the Purkinje fibers can send the signal back up to the atria
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The SA node sets its own pace of 60-100 impulses a minute; the AV delay isn't about recharging it.
    2. Right: the atria push blood into the ventricles first, and the pause lets the ventricles fill before they squeeze.
    3. Conduction through the AV node is slower at night, but the 0.1-second delay is part of every heartbeat, day or night.
    4. Purkinje fibers carry the signal through the ventricles, not back to the atria.

    The conduction path is SA node, AV node, Bundle of His, then Purkinje fibers. The short AV delay keeps the atria and ventricles contracting in the right order.

  3. Compared with the daytime, what happens to the heart at night?

    1. The gap between beats is longer, and conduction through the AV node and ventricles is slower
    2. The gap between beats is shorter, so the heart can rest between bursts
    3. Heart rate stays the same and only blood pressure changes
    4. Conduction through the AV node speeds up to make up for the slower SA node
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: heart rate is lowest at night, with longer gaps between beats and slower conduction.
    2. A shorter gap between beats would mean a faster heart rate, the opposite of what happens at night.
    3. Heart rate itself changes over 24 hours: highest in the day, lowest at night.
    4. Conduction through the AV node slows at night too; it doesn't speed up to compensate.

    Over a regular 24 hours, heart rate is highest during the day and lowest at night. The chapter notes that the gap between beats gets longer and conduction through the AV node and ventricles gets slower (Black).

  4. People on a forced non-24-hour schedule still had their lowest heart rates when they would normally have slept, but their heart rates were also lower during their actual, artificial sleep times. What does this suggest?

    1. Heart rate is set by sleep alone
    2. Heart rate is set by the internal clock alone, and sleep has no effect
    3. Heart rate is set only by light reaching the eyes
    4. Heart rate reflects a mixture of sleep patterns and an internal circadian clock
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. If sleep alone set it, heart rate wouldn't keep dropping at the old sleep times while people were awake.
    2. If the clock alone set it, actual sleep wouldn't lower heart rate as well, but it did.
    3. Light is one zeitgeber, but this study separated sleep from the clock; it didn't show light was the only factor.
    4. Right: both effects showed up, so both contribute.

    The study (Thosar) found a roughly 24-hour heart rate rhythm that persisted on an unnatural schedule, plus a separate drop during actual sleep. The chapter concludes that heart rate fluctuations come from both, with the SCN maintaining the 24-hour cycle.

  5. A runner trains at 6 p.m. every day for months. Based on the chapter, what might her cardiovascular system start to do?

    1. Nothing, because only light can act as a zeitgeber
    2. Raise heart rate and sympathetic activity slightly before 6 p.m., anticipating the workout
    3. Raise parasympathetic activity before 6 p.m. to save energy for the run
    4. Flatten out its daily heart rate rhythm completely
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Light is the best-known zeitgeber, but the chapter calls physical activity a nonphotic zeitgeber.
    2. Right: a regular session becomes a predictable daily event, so the heart is already ready to speed up.
    3. The parasympathetic system slows the heart; getting ready for exercise means a slight rise in sympathetic activity.
    4. Entrainment shapes the timing of the rhythm; nothing in the chapter says it removes the rhythm.

    The chapter describes this anticipatory effect as a plausible consequence of entrainment: exercise at the same time each day signals the body what time it is.

  6. Above the Arctic Circle, people did the same routine in the morning or the evening during a dark winter. Evening exercise delayed the peak of the heart rate rhythm compared with morning exercise and a control. What does this show?

    1. Daylight in the evening shifted the rhythm
    2. Evening exercise damages the heart's clock
    3. Exercise can act as a zeitgeber and shift the heart's daily rhythm
    4. Morning exercise stops the heart rate rhythm from peaking
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The study ran in a dark winter period, which is what makes light an unlikely explanation.
    2. A shifted peak is a change in timing, not evidence of harm.
    3. Right: with light largely removed, the timing of exercise itself moved the rhythm.
    4. The morning group still had a heart rate rhythm with a peak; it just wasn't delayed.

    The chapter cites "Morning or evening exercise: effects on the heart rate circadian rhythm above the Arctic Circle" as evidence that exercise timing can shift the heart's daily rhythm, which suggests exercise is a zeitgeber.

  7. Cardiac output falls by about 25% between ages 20 and 80. According to the chapter, what is mostly responsible?

    1. A lower resting heart rate
    2. A smaller amount of blood pushed out with each contraction
    3. A growing number of SA node cells that fire out of step
    4. A lower maximum heart rate
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Healthy aging doesn't generally change the resting heart rate; it's the maximum that drops.
    2. The amount per contraction stays roughly the same with age.
    3. SA node cells are lost with age: fewer than 10% remain by age 75.
    4. Right: cardiac output is heart rate times the amount per beat, and only the maximum heart rate falls much.

    Cardiac output equals heart rate multiplied by the blood pushed out with each contraction. Since the second stays roughly the same, the drop comes from peak heart rate, which falls by 25% between 20 and 80 as the SA node ages (Strait).

  8. Why can obesity make it harder for the heart to fill with blood?

    1. Its walls thicken from pumping more blood under higher pressure, and fat can cause stiff scar tissue
    2. There is less blood in the body, so there is less to fill the heart with
    3. Parasympathetic activity rises and stops the ventricles from relaxing
    4. The SA node fires too fast to leave time for filling
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: thicker walls leave less space for blood, and stiff scarring makes it harder to relax and fill.
    2. It's the reverse: obesity means extra blood in the body to reach the extra body fat.
    3. The chapter doesn't link obesity to more parasympathetic activity; the filling problem is structural.
    4. The chapter describes the problem as walls and scarring, not a fast-firing SA node.

    In obesity the heart pumps more every minute and under higher blood pressure, so its walls thicken. Fat in the heart releases signals that cause inflammation and damage, repaired with stiff scar tissue. These help explain higher heart failure rates (Wiley).

  9. Healthy day-shift workers did three day shifts and then three night shifts. What did the misalignment do to their hearts?

    1. Lowered blood pressure while awake, because they were more tired
    2. Increased parasympathetic activity while awake
    3. Had no effect, since three nights is too short to matter
    4. Raised blood pressure while awake and dampened the normal drop in systolic blood pressure during sleep
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Blood pressure went up during awake hours, not down.
    2. Parasympathetic activity, which protects the heart from working too hard, decreased significantly while awake.
    3. Even this short misalignment produced measurable changes.
    4. Right: waking blood pressure rose and the healthy nighttime dip was blunted.

    When the SCN's clock doesn't match the timing of sleep and daylight, the heart clock becomes less healthy (Chellappa). Even regular shift workers showed raised blood pressure, so they don't adapt, possibly because slow-changing melatonin and cortisol don't have time to adjust.

  10. A long-term smoker's heart rate variability (HRV) is measured. What does the chapter predict, and why?

    1. Higher HRV, because nicotine makes the heart change more from beat to beat
    2. Lower HRV, because smoking raises sympathetic and lowers parasympathetic activity
    3. Lower HRV, which is a sign of a calmer, healthier heart
    4. No change, because smoking affects the lungs rather than the nervous system
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. High HRV comes from the quick-acting parasympathetic system. Nicotine activates the sympathetic side instead.
    2. Right: the shift in balance away from the parasympathetic system lowers overall HRV.
    3. HRV does drop, but the chapter says low HRV means a less healthy, less adaptable heart.
    4. The chapter says smoking disrupts the autonomic nervous system, which controls heart rate.

    Both short- and long-term smoking increase sympathetic and decrease parasympathetic activity, lowering HRV. Nicotine is thought to be the main cause and may make the heart more vulnerable to dangerous arrhythmias (Dinas).

  11. A patient's SA node fires on time, but the signal cannot leave the node. What is this called?

    1. Sinus pause
    2. Sinus arrest
    3. SA exit block
    4. First-degree AV block
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. In sinus pause, the SA node fires late.
    2. In sinus arrest, the SA node fails to fire at all.
    3. Right: in SA exit block, the node fires but the signal is trapped inside it.
    4. An AV block happens further along, at the AV node, not at the SA node.

    The chapter lists three types of sinus node dysfunction (Desai). All make the heart beat too slowly or skip beats, so less blood reaches the brain, and patients usually need an artificial pacemaker.

  12. A patient's ventricles fill normally but they still have heart failure, and their ejection fraction is 55%. What is the most likely problem?

    1. Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, from damaged cells that can't squeeze
    2. Heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction
    3. It can't be heart failure, because the pump is working
    4. Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, usually from ventricles too stiff to relax and fill
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Reduced ejection fraction means 40% or less.
    2. Mildly reduced is 41-49%; 55% is above that.
    3. The chapter says heart failure can still occur at 50% or more, when the pump seems to work.
    4. Right: 50% or more is preserved, and the problem is usually stiff ventricles that can't fill between beats.

    Heart failure is classified by ejection fraction, the share of the heart's blood pushed out each beat (Shams). Reduced and mildly reduced forms usually come from damaged heart cells; the preserved form from stiffness.

  13. A patient is at risk of ventricular fibrillation. Which device would doctors use?

    1. An implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD)
    2. A single-chamber pacemaker
    3. VA-ECMO
    4. A dual-chamber pacemaker
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: an ICD watches for dangerous rhythms and delivers high-energy shocks to restore a normal one.
    2. Pacemakers treat a heart that beats too slowly, such as from sinus node dysfunction or AV block.
    3. VA-ECMO supports a heart too weak to pump; it doesn't correct a dangerous rhythm.
    4. A dual-chamber pacemaker coordinates the atria and ventricles but is still for a heart that beats too slowly.

    For more dangerous arrhythmias like ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation, the chapter says doctors use an ICD, which has a battery, a capacitor and a microprocessor, and can also work as a pacemaker (Sahu).

  14. A patient's heart is too weak to pump, and they may wait more than a year for a transplant. Which support fits best?

    1. VA-ECMO, which oxygenates the blood outside the body
    2. A left ventricular assist device (LVAD), such as the HeartMate 3
    3. An ICD, to shock the heart into pumping harder
    4. A pacemaker set to a faster pacing rate
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. VA-ECMO is temporary, used only for a few days to weeks, with risks like clots and vessel damage.
    2. Right: an LVAD gives long-term support, permanently or until a transplant.
    3. An ICD corrects dangerous rhythms; it doesn't make a weak heart pump more blood.
    4. A pacemaker fixes a beat that's too slow, not a pump that's too weak.

    Mechanical circulatory support devices are temporary (hours to weeks) or permanent (months or years). The HeartMate 3 pumps blood from the tip of the left ventricle into the aorta and had 2-year survival close to that after transplants.

  15. Which pairing matches the chapter?

    1. Sympathetic: brainstem and vagus nerve, slows the heart. Parasympathetic: thoracic spinal nerves, speeds it up.
    2. Sympathetic: acetylcholine on the SA node. Parasympathetic: norepinephrine on the SA node.
    3. Sympathetic: thoracic spinal nerves, speeds the heart up. Parasympathetic: brainstem and vagus nerve, slows it down.
    4. Both start in the thoracic spinal nerves and differ only in strength.
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. This swaps the two systems' locations and effects.
    2. This swaps the chemicals: the vagus nerve releases acetylcholine to slow the heart, and the sympathetic pathway releases norepinephrine.
    3. Right: each system has its own location, pathway and effect.
    4. The parasympathetic system is mainly in the brainstem, and the two have opposite effects.

    The sympathetic system is a group of 12 thoracic spinal nerves that triggers a flight-or-fight response. The parasympathetic system is mainly in the brainstem, uses the vagus nerve, and slows the heart.

  16. A student's heart rate jumps while they take a stressful math test. Which system is mainly responsible, and why does the body do this?

    1. Parasympathetic, because the student is sitting still
    2. Parasympathetic, to save energy for thinking
    3. Neither; the SCN raises heart rate directly at that time of day
    4. Sympathetic, a flight-or-fight response to get more oxygen and glucose to muscles and organs
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Sitting still would favor a lower heart rate; the rise points to the sympathetic system.
    2. The parasympathetic system conserves energy by slowing the heart, which is the opposite of what happened.
    3. The SCN sets the daily rhythm, but a sudden jump during stress comes from sympathetic stimulation of the SA node.
    4. Right: stress is a trigger for the sympathetic system, which stimulates the SA node to raise heart rate.

    The chapter says the many reasons for sympathetic stimulation, extrinsic and intrinsic, all stem from the body needing more oxygen and glucose sent to different muscles and organs.

  17. What do the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems have in common?

    1. Both slow the heart when we rest
    2. Both act only during exercise
    3. Both change heart rate through chemical signals on the SA node
    4. Both work by blocking the AV node
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Only the parasympathetic system slows the heart; the sympathetic system speeds it up.
    2. Their balance matters all the time, including at rest, and it shifts across the day.
    3. Right: they push in opposite directions on the same target, the heart's natural pacemaker.
    4. Neither works by blocking the AV node; AV block is a conduction problem.

    The chapter describes the two as opposing controls on how quickly the heart beats, both acting through chemical signalling on the SA node. The heart's rhythm depends on the balance between them.

  18. Which of these counts as endurance training in the chapter's sense?

    1. One all-out sprint at maximal heart rate
    2. Cycling several times a week for weeks, below maximal heart rate
    3. A single long swim once a year
    4. Sitting on a couch for five minutes before measuring heart rate
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Endurance training works at levels lower than maximal heart rate, and needs repetition.
    2. Right: repeated sessions below maximal heart rate, over weeks, are what make the heart adapt.
    3. One session is only an acute load; adaptation comes from repeated sessions.
    4. That's the chapter's way to see a resting heart rate, not a form of training.

    Endurance, or aerobic, training repeatedly works the heart below its maximal heart rate. A single session is an acute load; repeated over weeks, it becomes a stimulus that makes the heart adapt.

  19. After months of endurance training, a person's resting heart rate is lower. What is the main structural reason?

    1. The left ventricle can enlarge and fill with more blood, so each beat ejects more and fewer beats are needed
    2. The SA node loses cells, so it fires less often
    3. The heart walls stiffen, so each beat takes longer
    4. The AV node blocks some beats from reaching the ventricles
    Show the answer

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 · Walk through one heartbeat: where the electrical signal starts, how it travels, and how the valves keep blood moving the right way.

The SA node in the right atrium fires, telling the atria to contract and push blood into the ventricles. The AV node holds the signal for about 0.1 seconds so the ventricles can fill, then the Bundle of His and Purkinje fibers spread it through the ventricles so they contract together. Valves between the chambers and vessels, opened and closed mostly by pressure differences, stop blood from flowing backward. Heart rate is highest in the day and lowest at night.

LO2 · How do sleep, the SCN and outside cues like light or exercise work together to shape the heart's daily rhythm?

The SCN, the body's master clock, keeps heart rate on a roughly 24-hour cycle: high in the day, low at night. Studies on non-24-hour schedules show sleep lowers heart rate too, so the rhythm is a mix of the two. Zeitgebers set the clock through entrainment: light is the best known, but exercise is a nonphotic zeitgeber. Exercising at the same time daily may make the heart anticipate it, and evening exercise in an Arctic winter delayed the heart rate peak.

LO3 · Pick two of aging, obesity, shift work and smoking. How does each change the heart and its clock?

Aging lowers maximum heart rate, because the SA node loses cells and gathers fat, signals last longer and the heart responds less to adrenaline, so the heart can't keep up at peak effort. Obesity makes the heart pump more blood under higher pressure, thickening and scarring its walls and raising heart failure risk. Shift work misaligns the SCN, raising blood pressure and lowering parasympathetic activity. Smoking tips the balance toward the sympathetic system, lowering HRV and raising arrhythmia risk.

LO4 · Compare an electrical problem and a mechanical problem of the heart, and the device that would help each.

An electrical problem like sinus node dysfunction or a complete AV block makes the heart beat too slowly or skip beats, so less blood reaches the brain; a pacemaker restores the rhythm. Dangerous fast rhythms like ventricular fibrillation need an ICD, which shocks the heart back into rhythm. A mechanical problem like heart failure means the heart can't fill or push out blood properly, either because it can't squeeze or because it's too stiff to fill. A weak heart can get mechanical support: VA-ECMO for days to weeks, or an LVAD long term.

LO5 · Compare and contrast the sympathetic and parasympathetic control of the heart.

Both control how fast the heart beats by sending chemical signals to the SA node, and the heart's rhythm depends on the balance between them. The sympathetic system, in 12 thoracic spinal nerves, speeds the heart up for fight or flight when the body needs more oxygen and glucose. The parasympathetic system, mainly in the brainstem and working through the vagus nerve, slows it for rest and relaxation and saves energy. Parasympathetic effects act faster, which is why they give a higher heart rate variability.

LO6 · Explain to a friend why a trained runner can have a low resting heart rate.

Repeated endurance sessions below maximal heart rate, over weeks, make the heart adapt. The chambers, especially the left ventricle, enlarge and fill with more blood, so each beat pushes out a bigger stroke volume, and the heart can move the same blood with fewer beats and less strain. Training also shifts the resting balance toward the parasympathetic system, which slows the heart. That's why sinus bradycardia can even be normal in athletes.
