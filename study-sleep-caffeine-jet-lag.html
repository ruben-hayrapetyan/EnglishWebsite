The chapter in 18 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Introduction

An internal clock times sleep and waking

  • The sleep/wake cycle is cyclical and closely related to the circadian system.
  • An internal clock regulates it and generates circadian rhythms with a period of about 24 hours.
  • External cues, especially light, adjust the cycle to match the environment.
  • Light during the day promotes wakefulness; darkness in the evening helps the shift toward sleep.

1 · Introduction

Melatonin links light to sleep

  • Melatonin is a hormone that tells the body when to start preparing for sleep.
  • As light levels decrease, melatonin production increases. Light can inhibit or postpone it.
  • If melatonin secretion shifts, the sleep/wake cycle shifts with it.
  • Mistimed melatonin is associated with jet lag and other circadian sleep disturbances.

2 · Screen vs book before bed

An eReader at bedtime delays the clock

  • Chang et al. (2015): twelve healthy young adults read for about four hours before bed, from a light-emitting eReader and from a printed book in low light.
  • With the eReader: delayed circadian rhythm, longer time to fall asleep, less melatonin and lower alertness the next day.
  • Light from devices before bed can disrupt the natural sleep/wake cycle, at night and the next morning.

2 · What can disrupt the sleep/wake cycle?

Light, travel and schedules pull the clock off time

  • Artificial light late at night can shift circadian phase later while school or work still starts early, so sleep is cut short.
  • Jet lag: after rapidly crossing time zones, the clock stays in sync with the previous location until sunlight and other signals shift it.
  • Behavioral jet lag: very different sleep times on weekdays and weekends go with more circadian disruption and later melatonin.
  • Night shift work puts behavior, the light-dark cycle and circadian timing out of sync: circadian misalignment.

2 · What can disrupt the sleep/wake cycle?

Disruption reaches beyond tiredness

  • Daytime sleepiness and reduced alertness come first; over time, concentration, memory and everyday tasks can suffer.
  • Emotional experience varies across the wake-sleep cycle, so poor sleep can affect mood too.
  • Chronic disruption is linked to changes in immune, cardiovascular and metabolic systems, such as glucose metabolism.
  • One poor night doesn't necessarily do this harm; chronic or repeated disruption is more likely to.

2 · Maintaining a healthy sleep/wake cycle

Habits that keep the clock in sync

  • Keep a regular sleep/wake schedule across the week to minimize behavioral jet lag.
  • Get natural light during the day and limit device use and bright light before bed.
  • When traveling, allow time to adjust: the internal clock doesn't shift immediately.
  • Melatonin timing matters; taking it at any time won't necessarily improve sleep.

3 · Case study: Colgan Air Flight 3407

The accident

  • February 12, 2009: approaching Buffalo, the stick shaker warned of an aerodynamic stall as airspeed fell.
  • The captain pulled back on the control column, raising the angle of attack and worsening the stall.
  • The crash killed all 49 people aboard and one person on the ground.
  • The NTSB named the captain's inappropriate response to the stall warning as the probable cause.

3 · Case study: Colgan Air Flight 3407

Fatigue starts before the cockpit

  • The captain spent the night in the airline's crew room, with interruptions, noise, activity and light.
  • The first officer began a commute from Seattle about 15 hours before report time, with no chance of uninterrupted sleep.
  • Hours spent flying are only part of the risk: sleep before duty, time awake, commuting and biological timing all count.

3 · Case study: Colgan Air Flight 3407

Sleep pressure meets circadian timing

  • Sleep pressure rises the longer you're awake and falls with restorative sleep; poor sleep may have left the crew's pressure high.
  • Sleeping at irregular hours or while traveling can conflict with circadian signals.
  • Together they impair vigilance, attention and decision-making: the NTSB cited the crew's failure to monitor falling airspeed.
  • Gander et al. (2015) studied how sleep-wake history and circadian phase affect measures of pilot fatigue.

3 · Case study: Colgan Air Flight 3407

Scheduled rest is not the same as recovery

  • Rest only restores if sleep is long enough, continuous and free from disturbances.
  • Sleep can reduce sleep pressure without aligning internal timing with the environment.
  • The circadian push for wakefulness in the biological day can't fully make up for substantial sleep loss.
  • So the time between duty periods alone gives an incomplete picture of a pilot's state.

3 · Case study: Colgan Air Flight 3407

Beyond aviation

  • Small lab changes in reaction time or attention matter because flying needs them reliable for long periods.
  • The case links chronobiology to human factors: how human limits interact with equipment, environments and procedures.
  • Students studying late, shift workers and travelers face the same interplay of sleep pressure and circadian timing.
  • Sleep is a biological process, not just inactivity between responsibilities.

4 · Introduction to caffeine

What caffeine is, and why people use it

  • A widely consumed stimulant in the methylated xanthine group, found in more than 60 plant species.
  • It peaks in the blood about 45–60 minutes after consumption. In the US, coffee gives about 75% of intake, tea 15% and soda 10%.
  • People use it for alertness, habit, mood, social reasons, taste and to manage symptoms.
  • Heavy users report more morning tiredness: tired people may drink more, and caffeine may add to the fatigue.

5 · How caffeine affects the brain and body

Caffeine blocks adenosine and stretches the clock

  • Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the central nervous system, reducing drowsiness.
  • It is linked to improved memory retention and may lower serotonin availability.
  • In human cells it makes the circadian period longer, through adenosine receptors and cAMP signaling.
  • Its alerting effect may be more noticeable around midday, when sleepiness is greater, than in the evening.

6 · Caffeine timing and sleep quality

Morning, afternoon or evening, caffeine reaches sleep

  • Morning: the "coffee cycle". 200 mg at 7 a.m. still lowered sleep efficiency and total sleep time.
  • Afternoon: caffeine six hours before bed significantly disrupted sleep and moved REM earlier.
  • Evening: 100 mg right before sleep reduced delta waves, weakening deep sleep, and raised sleep spindles.
  • Evening caffeine also shifted the melatonin rhythm about 40 minutes later, delaying the clock.

7 · Applying research on caffeine timing to sleep habits

Dose and timing both matter

  • Gardiner et al. (2025): 100 mg did not significantly affect sleep; 400 mg disrupted it even 12 hours before bed.
  • Effects were stronger closer to bedtime. The sample was only 23 adult men, so the results may not generalize.
  • Effects can last long after a person stops feeling more alert, so consider when you drink caffeine, not just how much.

8 · Jet lag and internal desynchrony

When the master clock falls out of step

  • The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus is the body's master clock.
  • Internal desynchrony: this clock is out of sync with the rest of the body or the environment.
  • Jet lag is a well-known form: after rapid travel across time zones, external cues don't match internal clocks.
  • Sleep is the best-known casualty, but other functions can take weeks to months to readjust.

8 · Jet lag and internal desynchrony

Jet lag uncouples Process S and Process C

  • Process S builds sleep pressure as adenosine accumulates while awake; sleep resets it.
  • Process C, run by the SCN, times alertness and drowsiness: cortisol and serotonin by day, melatonin as night falls.
  • Process S is mostly unaffected by time zones; Process C follows local time and the SCN, and realigns only slowly.
  • Local night but old-zone morning: wake signals are high and adenosine is far below the sleep threshold.

8 · Jet lag and internal desynchrony

Damaged sleep architecture spreads to other systems

  • Sleep architecture is the cycling through REM and NREM stages; jet lag fragments it.
  • REM, which depends heavily on Process C, is hit hardest.
  • Disrupted REM went with poorer insulin activity and sensitivity, timed badly against food intake.
  • Attention, working and short-term memory, executive function and emotional regulation also suffer.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Behavioral jet lag
A shift in behaviors such as sleeping, waking, or eating at markedly different times between weekdays and weekends.
Circadian misalignment
When behavior, the environmental light-dark cycle, and circadian timing are not in sync, as for a night shift worker. Also known as circadian disruption.
Stick shaker
A warning system that physically vibrates the pilots' control columns when the aircraft approaches an aerodynamic stall.
Aerodynamic stall
Occurs when the angle between an aircraft's wings and the oncoming airflow, known as the angle of attack, exceeds a critical threshold, causing airflow separation and a substantial reduction in lift.
Human factors
The ways in which human abilities and limitations interact with equipment, environments, and procedures.
Caffeine
A widely consumed stimulant, found in coffee and other beverages, that can improve performance and alertness while reducing tiredness.
Circadian rhythm
A self-sustaining biological rhythm that follows an approximately 24-hour cycle and continues even without external time cues. It regulates processes such as sleep, wakefulness, body temperature, and hormone release.
Sleep efficiency
The percentage of time spent in bed that a person is actually asleep.
Sleep-wake cycle
An approximately 24-hour biological cycle that regulates periods of sleep and wakefulness, primarily influenced by the circadian pacemaker, the body's internal biological clock.
Adenosine
A chemical produced through cellular metabolism that acts as a neuromodulator in the nervous system and regulates sleep. Adenosine builds up during wakefulness, increasing feelings of sleepiness, and decreases during sleep.
Jet lag (circadian desynchrony)
A temporary disturbance to the natural sleep/wake cycle (and other physiological processes) due to travel across time zones.
Internal desynchrony
When your internal biological clocks are out of sync with each other and/or the environment.
Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)
The biological master clock of the human body, located in the hypothalamus of the brain.
Process S
Homeostatic pressure input (sleep homeostasis): it builds sleep pressure the longer you stay awake, through the accumulation of adenosine in the brain.
Process C
Circadian pacemaker: controlled by the SCN, it uses hormones and chemical signals to time drowsiness and alertness, independent of how long you've been awake.
Melatonin
A natural hormone produced in the pineal gland of the brain responsible for inducing drowsiness in preparation for sleep.
Sleep architecture
The cycling through different stages of sleep, critical to proper rest.

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 and LO2 · How do the internal clock, light and melatonin work together to tell you when to sleep?

An internal clock generates circadian rhythms with a period of about 24 hours and regulates the sleep/wake cycle. Light adjusts that clock to the environment: daylight promotes wakefulness, and darkness in the evening lets melatonin production rise. Melatonin tells the body to prepare for sleep, so when its secretion is on time the shift from wake to sleep happens properly. Light at night suppresses or delays melatonin, and a shift in melatonin shifts the whole sleep/wake cycle.

LO3 and LO4 · Pick two everyday things that disrupt the sleep/wake cycle. How do they affect daily life, and what habits counter them?

Late-night device light shifts circadian phase later and lowers melatonin, but school or work still starts early, so people sleep too little and feel sleepy and unfocused. Sleeping in on weekends creates behavioral jet lag, linked to more circadian disruption and later melatonin. Over time disruption can affect memory, mood and even immune and metabolic health, though one bad night usually doesn't. The fixes: keep the same schedule all week, get daylight during the day, limit bright screens before bed, and time melatonin carefully.

LO5 and LO8 · Using the Colgan Air pilots, explain why time off duty doesn't guarantee an alert pilot.

The captain spent the night in a crew room with noise, light and interruptions, and the first officer commuted overnight from Seattle with no chance of uninterrupted sleep. Both had some sleep loss, so their sleep pressure may not have fully dissipated, and sleeping at irregular hours or while traveling conflicts with circadian signals. Sleep can lower sleep pressure without aligning internal timing, and daytime circadian alertness can't fully make up for substantial sleep loss. Recovery needs sleep that is long, continuous and undisturbed, not just scheduled rest.

LO6 and LO7 · How does pilot fatigue research connect to aviation safety and human factors?

Lab studies, like Gander et al.'s work on sleep-wake history and circadian phase, measure things like reaction time and sustained attention. These changes can look small, but flying needs them to stay reliable for long periods under complex, changing conditions. The Colgan crew had to track airspeed, configuration, instruments, weather and procedures at once, and the NTSB cited their failure to monitor falling airspeed. That makes fatigue a human factors issue: how human abilities and limits interact with equipment, environments and procedures.

LO9 and LO10 · Where does caffeine come from, why do people use it, and what does it do in the brain?

Caffeine is a methylated xanthine found in more than 60 plants, mainly coffee beans, tea leaves, kola nuts and cocoa beans; in the US coffee gives about 75% of intake. People use it for alertness, habit, mood, social reasons, taste and symptoms. It blocks adenosine receptors in the central nervous system, which reduces drowsiness, and may lower serotonin availability. It also lengthens the circadian period in human cells, and its alerting effect may be stronger around midday than in the evening.

LO11 and LO12 · A friend says coffee only matters if you drink it right before bed. How would you correct them?

Caffeine affects sleep at every time of day. 200 mg at 7 a.m. still lowered sleep efficiency and total sleep time, and morning tiredness can start a "coffee cycle". Caffeine six hours before bed significantly disrupted sleep. Right before bed, it reduced deep-sleep delta waves and delayed the melatonin rhythm by about 40 minutes. Dose matters too: in Gardiner et al., 400 mg disrupted sleep even 12 hours before bed, while 100 mg didn't significantly, though that study only had 23 men. So think about when and how much.

LO13 and LO15 · Describe how a graph of Process S and Process C changes after a long flight, and how it shows a mistimed rhythm.

Normally Process S rises with time awake as adenosine builds and drops back during sleep, while Process C rises and falls with the SCN's daily cycle; sleep comes when the two line up. After rapid travel, Process S is mostly unaffected by time zones but Process C stays tied to the old zone, so its curve is shifted against local time. You can spot the mistiming because hormones and chemical signals, such as serotonin, peak at the wrong time of day. Environmental cues gradually pull Process C back, so the curves line up again over time.

LO14 and LO16 · How can jet lag spread beyond sleep, and how well do the ways of recovering work?

Jet lag fragments sleep architecture, especially REM, which depends on Process C. Reduced REM and misalignment disrupted insulin activity and sensitivity, poorly timed against meals, and poor sleep also reduces attention, memory, executive function and emotional regulation. The body recovers on its own as environmental cues, especially sunlight, slowly realign the clock, but some functions can take weeks to months. Melatonin can help only if taken at the right time relative to the internal clock; at the wrong time it shifts the clock in an unwanted direction.
