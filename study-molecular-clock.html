The basics of biorhythms: the molecular clock

How a loop of genes and proteins keeps a roughly 24-hour beat in every cell, how the SCN coordinates clocks across the body, and how serotonin feeds into the clock and then becomes melatonin.

The chapter in 18 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Serotonin

How the body builds serotonin

  • Serotonin (5-HT) is a neurotransmitter: a chemical messenger one neuron releases to activate or quiet another.
  • It is built from the amino acid tryptophan, obtained through food.
  • Step 1: tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH) makes 5-HTP. Step 2: aromatic amino acid decarboxylase (AADC) turns 5-HTP into serotonin.
  • The first step is slower and sets the pace, so TPH is the rate-limiting enzyme.

1 · Serotonin

The brain’s own supply

  • About 90% of serotonin is made in the gut.
  • Blood serotonin does not easily cross the blood-brain barrier, so the brain relies on its own smaller supply.
  • That supply comes mainly from the raphe nuclei along the brainstem midline, chiefly the dorsal and median raphe.

1 · Serotonin

Serotonin release has a daily rhythm

  • In hamster SCN, release was lowest in the late midday, peaked sharply at the light-to-dark transition, then declined through the night (Dudley et al., 1998).
  • The cycle persisted in constant darkness, peaking at the start of subjective night: the clock itself, not light, set its timing.
  • Raphe neurons may keep time too: clock genes cycle there in male rats, but that single study needs further verification.

1 · Serotonin

Two routes into the clock

  • Left alone, the SCN runs about 24.2 hours in humans; zeitgebers such as sunlight and mealtimes reset it.
  • Light is the strongest cue, but serotonin is another input.
  • In hamsters, the median raphe projects directly to the SCN, while the dorsal raphe projects to the intergeniculate leaflet (IGL), which relays nonphotic information to the SCN.

1 · Serotonin

Forward by day

  • A phase advance moves the clock earlier; a delay moves it later. A phase response curve plots shift against the time the stimulus is given.
  • In circadian time, CT0 to CT12 is subjective day and CT12 to CT24 subjective night.
  • The serotonin-receptor drug 8-OH-DPAT given mid subjective day advanced hamster clocks, and at CT6 advanced mouse wheel-running by about 37 minutes: nudges, not a shove.

1 · Serotonin

Gating by night

  • At night serotonin reduces the clock’s sensitivity to light instead of shifting it.
  • 8-OH-DPAT cut the advance from a nighttime light pulse by 57 percent at a low dose and 82 percent at a dose ten times higher (Rea, Glass, & Colwell, 1994).
  • The higher dose also blocked light-induced delays: the clock was made harder to move.
  • Serotonin acts as a forward signal by day and a gate at night.

1 · Serotonin

The 5-HT7 receptor

  • 8-OH-DPAT acts on more than one serotonin receptor, but in mice the evidence points to 5-HT7 for the daytime advance.
  • 5-HT7 raises cyclic AMP (cAMP), a messenger that relays the signal inside the cell.
  • Blocking 5-HT7 fully prevented the 8-OH-DPAT advance and weakened light-induced shifts (Shelton et al., 2014).

1 · Serotonin

From serotonin to melatonin

  • In the pineal gland, serotonin is a reactant: AANAT turns it into N-acetylserotonin, which a second enzyme turns into melatonin.
  • AANAT activity rises and falls daily, so the rhythm comes from regulating the enzyme, not from the amount of serotonin. Klein called it “the Timezyme”.
  • At night, rising cAMP switches AANAT on and protects it; in rats AANAT mRNA rises more than 150-fold.
  • The SCN’s signal travels down the spinal cord, out to nerve cells in the neck and back up to the pineal, where norepinephrine is released at night.

1 · Serotonin

What remains unsettled

  • In hamsters and mice, serotonergic drugs act as nonphotic signals that advance the clock by day; in rats they act more like light, shifting it at night. This is the serotonergic paradox.
  • Method matters: serotonin on isolated mouse SCN slices (in vitro) advances the clock two to three hours, versus about 37 minutes in living mice (in vivo).
  • Mice that could not make brain serotonin still kept a daily rhythm, though a transformed one: serotonin adjusts the clock rather than being its source.

2 · The molecular clock and circadian rhythms

A clock inside each cell

  • The molecular clock is an internal cellular mechanism that helps create circadian timing.
  • It is a cycle of genes and proteins whose activity level changes throughout the day.
  • These changes can affect neuronal activity, hormone release and behavioral timing.

3 · Central and local clocks in the brain and body

The SCN, the master pacemaker

  • The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) sits in the hypothalamus, directly above where the optic nerves cross.
  • Specialized retinal cells send light signals to it through the retinohypothalamic tract.
  • The SCN syncs internal rhythms with the light-dark cycle and helps regulate sleep, activity, body temperature and hormone release.

3 · Central and local clocks in the brain and body

Many cell clocks, one signal

  • Individual SCN neurons contain molecular clocks; the clocks regulate neuronal activity, and activity can in turn influence clock gene expression.
  • VIP helps synchronize the cell clocks into a stronger, more coordinated signal.
  • GABA also influences synchronization and, depending on conditions, may support or reduce it.

3 · Central and local clocks in the brain and body

Local clocks

  • Local clocks sit in other brain regions and organs such as the liver, heart, pancreas and skeletal muscles.
  • They time tissue functions: clocks in metabolic organs help regulate digestion and glucose levels.
  • The SCN uses light as its main cue; local clocks use a combination of feeding, hormones, temperature and nervous-system signals.

4 · Genetic mechanisms of the molecular clock

CLOCK and BMAL1 start the cycle

  • The clock is a transcription-translation feedback loop: DNA is copied into RNA, and RNA is used to build protein.
  • CLOCK and BMAL1 pair into a heterodimer in the nucleus and act as a transcription factor that turns on Per and Cry.
  • Without them, especially BMAL1, the clock breaks down.
  • As pioneer-like transcription factors, they open packed chromatin so thousands of other genes can be expressed at set times.

4 · Genetic mechanisms of the molecular clock

PER and CRY close the loop

  • PER and CRY proteins slowly collect, form complexes, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK and BMAL1.
  • Products inhibiting the process that made them is negative feedback.
  • When PER and CRY are modified and degraded, CLOCK and BMAL1 reactivate and the cycle repeats.
  • Delays throughout make the cycle approximately 24 hours.

4 · Genetic mechanisms of the molecular clock

Extra loops and tissue-specific output

  • REV-ERB suppresses Bmal1 and ROR activates it; they compete for the same binding site, a push-and-pull switch.
  • Clock-controlled genes don’t make the clock but let it influence other cell functions.
  • The same core loop drives different genes in different tissues: nutrient processing in a liver cell, electrical activity in a neuron.

5 · Clock genes and cyclic behavior

What mutant mice show, and don’t

  • Clock mutant mice had a weaker feeding rhythm, and many developed obesity and metabolic issues (Turek et al., 2005).
  • But the mutation also affects metabolism, which could itself change appetite, and light can drive activity directly.
  • So behavior must be tracked over several days and under many conditions.
  • Mice lacking Bmal1 only in the nervous system had trouble adjusting activity and feeding, avoiding whole-body developmental issues (Mieda & Sakurai, 2011).

6 · Conclusion

One system of genes, brain, body and environment

  • The CLOCK–BMAL1 and PER–CRY negative feedback loop generates an approximately 24-hour cycle.
  • Light reaches the SCN through the eyes, and the SCN coordinates local clocks throughout the brain and body.
  • Clock and Bmal1 mutants link clock genes to activity and feeding, but health, development and metabolism must be weighed first.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Serotonin (5-HT)
A neurotransmitter made from the amino acid tryptophan. In the brain it comes mostly from the raphe nuclei, though most of the body’s serotonin is made in the gut.
Raphe nuclei
Clusters of neurons along the midline of the brainstem that produce most of the brain’s serotonin. The dorsal and median raphe are the two that connect to the circadian system.
Zeitgeber
An external time cue, such as light, that resets (entrains) the circadian clock to the 24-hour day.
Phase response curve (PRC)
A graph showing how far, and in which direction, a clock shifts when a stimulus is applied at different times in the circadian cycle.
Phase advance / phase delay
A shift of the circadian clock to an earlier time (advance) or a later time (delay).
Circadian time (CT)
A 24-part scale for position within the cycle. CT0 to CT12 is the subjective day and CT12 to CT24 the subjective night; for a nocturnal animal, CT12 marks the start of activity.
AANAT (arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase)
The pineal enzyme, nicknamed “the Timezyme,” that converts serotonin into N-acetylserotonin. Because its activity rises at night, it sets the daily rhythm of melatonin.
Melatonin
A hormone made from serotonin in the pineal gland. Its levels rise at night, signaling biological darkness to the rest of the body.
Pineal gland
A small gland near the center of the brain that converts serotonin into melatonin, mainly at night.
In vitro / in vivo
In vitro means in an isolated preparation, such as a slice of brain tissue; in vivo means in the living animal.
Molecular clock
A system of interacting genes and proteins that generates biological timing within a cell.
Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)
A region of the hypothalamus that receives light information from the eyes and coordinates circadian rhythms in mammals.
Vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP)
A signaling peptide that helps coordinate the timing of individual cellular clocks in the SCN.
Local clock
A molecular clock in a particular brain region, organ, or tissue that helps time functions in that location.
Transcription-translation feedback loop
A cycle in which genes are transcribed into RNA, the RNA is translated into proteins, and those proteins influence further gene transcription.
Pioneer-like transcription factor
A transcription factor that can bind DNA in relatively inaccessible chromatin and help make that region accessible to other regulatory proteins.
Negative feedback
A process in which the products of a system reduce the activity that produced them.
REV-ERB
A family of nuclear receptor proteins that repress transcription of genes such as Bmal1 and help regulate circadian timing.
ROR
A family of nuclear receptor proteins that activate transcription of genes such as Bmal1 and help regulate circadian timing.
Clock-controlled gene
A gene whose expression is regulated by the circadian clock and helps produce timed cellular or bodily functions.

  1. Which description best captures how the molecular clock relates to circadian rhythms?

    1. It is a light sensor in the eye that switches the body between day and night modes
    2. It is a cycle of genes and proteins inside cells whose changing activity helps time neuronal activity, hormone release and behavior
    3. It is a single hormone that rises at night and tells every organ what time it is
    4. It is a record of past sleep that the brain consults to decide when to sleep next
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Specialized retinal cells detect light and signal the SCN, but they are an input to the clock, not the molecular clock itself.
    2. Right: the chapter defines the molecular clock as a cellular cycle of genes and proteins whose activity changes across the day and can affect neuronal activity, hormone release and behavioral timing.
    3. That sounds like melatonin, which is one output of the clock system, not the mechanism that generates timing.
    4. The chapter describes no such memory; the clock is a self-repeating gene and protein loop.

    The molecular clock is an internal cellular mechanism: genes and proteins whose activity levels rise and fall over the day, and those changes shape the body’s daily rhythms.

  2. According to the chapter’s conclusion, what is the division of labor between the core feedback loop and cues such as feeding and light?

    1. Feeding and light generate the cycle, and the feedback loop only copies their timing
    2. The feedback loop only works in the SCN, while feeding and light run every other tissue
    3. Neither matters much; the rhythm comes mostly from serotonin levels in the blood
    4. The CLOCK–BMAL1 and PER–CRY loop generates the roughly 24-hour cycle, while feeding and light help maintain its timing
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. This reverses the roles: the loop produces the cycle, and cues keep it aligned.
    2. Cells throughout the body use the same core loop; it isn’t limited to the SCN.
    3. Blood serotonin barely crosses into the brain, and mice without brain serotonin still kept a daily rhythm.
    4. Right: the conclusion says the feedback loop generates the cycle and additional regulators such as feeding and environmental light help maintain its timing.

    The molecular clock produces the rhythm; external cues regulate it so it stays in step with the day.

  3. A drug slows tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH) but leaves AADC untouched. What happens to serotonin production?

    1. It slows, because the TPH step is the slower, rate-limiting step that sets the pace
    2. Nothing changes, because AADC is the step that makes serotonin directly
    3. It speeds up, because 5-HTP no longer has to wait for AADC
    4. The body skips the step and turns tryptophan straight into serotonin
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: TPH converts tryptophan to 5-HTP, and because that first step is slower it sets the pace of the whole process.
    2. AADC does make serotonin, but only from 5-HTP, which TPH must supply first.
    3. Slowing the first step leaves less 5-HTP for AADC, so production can’t speed up.
    4. The chapter describes no shortcut: tryptophan must become 5-HTP before it becomes serotonin.

    Serotonin is built in two steps, tryptophan to 5-HTP (TPH) and 5-HTP to serotonin (AADC). TPH is the rate-limiting enzyme.

  4. About 90% of the body’s serotonin is made in the gut. Why does the brain’s circadian system still depend on the raphe nuclei?

    1. Gut serotonin is a different molecule from brain serotonin
    2. The raphe nuclei store gut serotonin and release it at night
    3. Blood serotonin does not easily cross the blood-brain barrier, so the brain relies on its own supply from the raphe
    4. The gut stops making serotonin during sleep
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. It is the same molecule; what differs is where it can reach. TPH comes in a gut form and a brain form, but both lead to serotonin.
    2. The raphe nuclei make serotonin themselves; they don’t store gut serotonin.
    3. Right: the blood-brain barrier keeps blood serotonin out, so the brain uses its own smaller supply from neurons of the raphe nuclei.
    4. The chapter says nothing about the gut stopping production, and it wouldn’t explain why the brain needs its own source.

    The raphe nuclei, clusters of neurons along the brainstem midline, supply the brain’s serotonin, and the dorsal and median raphe connect it to the clock.

  5. Hamsters kept in constant darkness still showed a peak in SCN serotonin release at the start of their subjective night. What does this tell you?

    1. Darkness itself triggers serotonin release
    2. The timing of release is set by the animal’s own clock, not by light signals
    3. The rhythm is a leftover that fades within one cycle
    4. Serotonin release is constant, and the peak was a measurement error
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. In constant darkness there is no light-to-dark change to trigger anything, yet the peak still came on schedule.
    2. Right: with no light to signal the time of day, the peak still tracked subjective night, so the clock itself set the timing.
    3. The chapter says the cycle persisted; it gives no sign of it fading.
    4. Dudley et al. (1998) found a clear rise and fall, so release isn’t constant.

    Persistence without light cues is the signature of an internal clock: serotonin reaches the SCN as a rhythmic supply, not a background one.

  6. Under a normal light-dark cycle, when is serotonin release in the hamster SCN highest?

    1. In the late midday
    2. In the middle of the night
    3. Just before dawn
    4. At the transition from light to darkness
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Late midday is the low point, not the peak.
    2. Release declines through the night after the peak.
    3. By dawn release has been falling all night.
    4. Right: release rose sharply to its peak at the light-to-dark transition (Dudley et al., 1998).

    Release is lowest in the late midday, peaks as light turns to darkness, then falls through the night. Section 3 links this peak to serotonin’s nighttime gating of light.

  7. A mouse receives 8-OH-DPAT at CT6. What should happen to the start of its daily wheel-running?

    1. It shifts several hours later
    2. It shifts earlier by a modest amount, about 37 minutes
    3. It is unaffected, because serotonin only acts at night
    4. It shifts earlier by about half a day
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. That is a delay; during the subjective day serotonergic signaling advances the clock.
    2. Right: CT6 is mid subjective day, and the drug advanced wheel-running onset by about 37 minutes (Shelton et al., 2014).
    3. Serotonin does act at night, as a gate on light, but by day it advances the clock.
    4. The chapter calls these shifts nudges rather than a meaningful shove.

    CT0 to CT12 is subjective day, the most relevant window for serotonin. A serotonergic signal then produces a small phase advance.

  8. A hamster gets a high dose of 8-OH-DPAT, then a light pulse at night. Compared with light alone, what happens to the clock?

    1. The light shifts the clock even further than usual
    2. The drug itself shifts the clock several hours earlier
    3. The light-induced shift is much smaller: the clock becomes harder to move
    4. The light shift turns from an advance into a delay
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Serotonin reduces the clock’s sensitivity to light at night; it doesn’t amplify it.
    2. At night serotonin acts as a gate rather than shifting the clock itself.
    3. Right: the higher dose cut the light-induced advance by 82 percent and also blocked light-induced delays (Rea, Glass, & Colwell, 1994).
    4. The drug weakened or blocked light shifts in both directions; it didn’t reverse them.

    Serotonin is a forward signal by day and a gate at night, and its nighttime peak in the SCN fits that gating role.

  9. In hamsters, how does serotonin from the dorsal raphe reach the SCN?

    1. Directly, through its own fibers into the SCN
    2. Through the intergeniculate leaflet, which relays nonphotic information to the SCN
    3. Through the blood, from serotonin made in the gut
    4. Through the retinohypothalamic tract, along with light signals
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. That is the median raphe’s route; the dorsal raphe route is relayed.
    2. Right: dorsal raphe neurons project to the IGL in the thalamus, which passes non-light information on to the SCN (Meyer-Bernstein & Morin, 1996).
    3. Blood serotonin does not easily cross the blood-brain barrier.
    4. The retinohypothalamic tract carries light information from the retina, not serotonin from the raphe.

    Serotonin has two routes into the clock: direct from the median raphe, and relayed through the IGL from the dorsal raphe.

  10. Serotonin is available in the pineal gland around the clock, yet melatonin is made mainly at night. What explains the rhythm?

    1. Serotonin is only delivered to the pineal gland at night
    2. Melatonin is made in the SCN and only released by the pineal gland at night
    3. The second, methylating enzyme only exists at night
    4. The activity of AANAT, the first enzyme, rises at night and sets the melatonin rhythm
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The chapter says the rhythm comes from regulating the enzyme, not from the amount of serotonin available.
    2. Melatonin is made in the pineal gland; the SCN only sends the instructions.
    3. The chapter singles out the first step, AANAT, as the one that matters for circadian timing.
    4. Right: AANAT, “the Timezyme”, rises and falls through the day, switched on at night by rising cAMP.

    Serotonin becomes N-acetylserotonin (AANAT), then melatonin (a second enzyme). Regulating AANAT turns a steady supply into a hormonal rhythm.

  11. Which sequence traces the SCN’s nighttime signal to make melatonin?

    1. SCN, a chain of neurons down into the spinal cord, nerve cells in the neck, then norepinephrine at the pineal, raising cAMP
    2. SCN, then serotonin released straight onto the pineal gland, which lowers cAMP
    3. Retina, the pineal gland directly, then the SCN
    4. SCN, the raphe nuclei, then melatonin released into the gut
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: the signal leaves the brain, runs down the spinal cord, out to a cluster of nerve cells in the neck and back up to the pineal, where norepinephrine raises cAMP (Simonneaux & Ribelayga, 2003).
    2. The messenger at the pineal is norepinephrine, and it raises cAMP rather than lowering it.
    3. The instructions come from the SCN, not straight from the retina.
    4. The raphe nuclei supply serotonin to the SCN; they are not the route to the pineal.

    The instructions don’t come from the pineal itself: they travel from the SCN on a long route out of the brain and back, ending with norepinephrine at night.

  12. A serotonergic drug shifts a rat’s clock mainly at night, while in hamsters it advances the clock by day. What is this mismatch called?

    1. The rate-limiting effect
    2. Phase gating
    3. The serotonergic paradox
    4. The 5-HT7 reversal
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. “Rate-limiting” describes TPH in serotonin synthesis, not a species difference.
    2. Gating is serotonin’s nighttime dampening of light responses, not a disagreement between species.
    3. Right: in hamsters and mice serotonergic drugs act as nonphotic signals, while in rats they behave more like light, a mismatch known as the serotonergic paradox (Cuesta et al., 2009).
    4. The chapter names no such effect; 5-HT7 is the receptor behind the daytime advance in mice.

    Much of the disagreement about serotonin’s role goes back to which animals were studied.

  13. Serotonin on isolated mouse SCN slices advances the clock two to three hours; a drug in living mice advances it about 37 minutes. What is the best conclusion?

    1. One of the two studies must have measured wrongly
    2. The method of study matters: in vitro and in vivo preparations can give very different sizes of effect
    3. Slices prove serotonin is the main source of the clock’s rhythm
    4. Living mice have no serotonin receptors in the SCN
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The chapter treats the gap as a real difference between methods, not an error.
    2. Right: Prosser (2003) worked in vitro and Shelton et al. (2014) in vivo, and the chapter uses the gap to show that the method of study matters.
    3. Mice that couldn’t make brain serotonin still kept a daily rhythm, so serotonin adjusts the clock rather than being its source.
    4. The 37-minute advance in living mice depended on 5-HT7 receptors, so they clearly have them.

    Species and method both shape the results, which is why serotonin’s role in the clock remains nondefinitive.

  14. Mice are fed only at a fixed time each day. Which clock is this cue most directly aimed at?

    1. The SCN, because it uses feeding as its main timing cue
    2. None, because only light can reset a circadian clock
    3. The retina, which then passes feeding time to the SCN
    4. Local clocks such as the liver’s, which use feeding, hormones and temperature as cues
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The SCN’s main cue is light, not feeding.
    2. Zeitgebers include mealtimes as well as light, and local clocks use a mix of cues.
    3. The retina detects light; it doesn’t sense feeding.
    4. Right: local clocks use a combination of stimuli, including feeding, and clocks in metabolic organs help regulate digestion and glucose.

    The main difference between the SCN and local clocks is the cue: the SCN relies on light, local clocks on feeding, hormones, temperature and nervous-system signals.

  15. What makes the SCN’s many cellular clocks produce one strong, coordinated signal?

    1. Communication among SCN neurons, with VIP helping synchronize the individual clocks
    2. Each SCN neuron lacks its own clock and simply follows the retina
    3. Local clocks in the liver send timing signals back up to the SCN
    4. GABA always forces all SCN neurons into the same phase
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: SCN neurons communicate chemically and electrically, and VIP plays an important role in synchronizing the cellular clocks (Herzog et al., 2017).
    2. Individual SCN neurons do contain molecular clocks.
    3. The chapter describes the SCN coordinating local clocks, not the other way round.
    4. GABA can support or reduce synchronization depending on conditions, so it doesn’t always force it.

    The SCN is a master pacemaker built from many cell clocks, which neurotransmitters such as VIP and GABA tie together into one rhythm.

  16. PER and CRY proteins build up and enter the nucleus. What happens next?

    1. They activate CLOCK and BMAL1, speeding up Per and Cry transcription
    2. They inhibit CLOCK and BMAL1, so Per and Cry transcription slows
    3. They are translated into new Per and Cry genes
    4. They pair up to form the CLOCK–BMAL1 heterodimer
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. That would be positive feedback; the loop is negative feedback.
    2. Right: PER and CRY inhibit CLOCK and BMAL1, slowing the transcription that made them, which is negative feedback.
    3. Genes aren’t made from proteins; translation builds proteins from RNA.
    4. CLOCK and BMAL1 are separate proteins that form their own pair.

    CLOCK–BMAL1 turns on Per and Cry; PER and CRY then shut CLOCK–BMAL1 down until they are degraded.

  17. Suppose PER and CRY proteins could no longer be degraded. What would most likely happen to the cycle?

    1. It would run faster, finishing in about 12 hours
    2. It would be unaffected, because degradation plays no role in the loop
    3. It would stall, because CLOCK and BMAL1 could not reactivate to restart transcription
    4. It would switch to being driven by ROR alone
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Removing a step that releases the brake wouldn’t speed the cycle up.
    2. The chapter says the cycle repeats only as PER and CRY are modified and degraded.
    3. Right: degradation lets CLOCK and BMAL1 reactivate and resume transcription, so without it the inhibition would persist.
    4. ROR helps regulate Bmal1 in an additional loop; it doesn’t replace the core loop.

    Breakdown of PER and CRY is what resets the loop. The steps and their delays make the cycle approximately 24 hours.

  18. At a time of day when REV-ERB levels are high, what should happen to BMAL1?

    1. BMAL1 levels rise, because REV-ERB activates the Bmal1 gene
    2. BMAL1 levels fall, because REV-ERB blocks Bmal1 transcription
    3. BMAL1 levels stay the same, because REV-ERB acts only on Per
    4. BMAL1 is converted into ROR
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. That is ROR’s job; REV-ERB suppresses Bmal1.
    2. Right: REV-ERB and ROR compete for the same binding site, and high REV-ERB blocks transcription and lowers BMAL1 (Guillaumond et al., 2005).
    3. The chapter describes REV-ERB acting on the Bmal1 gene.
    4. ROR is a separate protein that competes with REV-ERB; BMAL1 isn’t turned into it.

    REV-ERB and ROR form a push-and-pull switch that turns Bmal1 on and off at set times, stabilizing the core loop.

  19. Clock mutant mice show a weaker feeding rhythm and often become obese. Why can’t we conclude that Clock directly controls all feeding behavior?

    1. The mutation also changes metabolism, which could itself alter appetite and eating habits
    2. Clock is not a gene involved in the circadian system
    3. The mice were only observed for a single day in constant darkness

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 and LO8 · Walk through one full turn of the molecular clock, and say how it produces a circadian rhythm.

CLOCK and BMAL1 pair up in the nucleus and act as a transcription factor that turns on the Per and Cry genes. Their mRNA is used to build PER and CRY proteins, which slowly collect, form complexes, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK and BMAL1. That slows Per and Cry transcription: negative feedback. When PER and CRY are modified and degraded, CLOCK and BMAL1 reactivate and the cycle starts again. The delays make each turn approximately 24 hours, and because gene and protein activity changes across the day, it can time neuronal activity, hormone release and behavior. REV-ERB and ROR add a push-and-pull loop on Bmal1 that stabilizes the cycle.

LO2 · What is serotonin, how is it made, and where does the brain get it?

Serotonin, or 5-HT, is a neurotransmitter: a messenger one neuron releases to activate or quiet another. It is built from tryptophan in food. Tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH) turns tryptophan into 5-HTP, and AADC turns 5-HTP into serotonin. TPH’s step is slower, so it is the rate-limiting enzyme. About 90% of serotonin is made in the gut, but blood serotonin doesn’t easily cross the blood-brain barrier, so the brain uses its own supply from the raphe nuclei on the brainstem midline, mainly the dorsal and median raphe.

LO3 and LO4 · Describe serotonin’s daily rhythm in the SCN, how it gets there, and what it does by day and by night.

In hamsters, SCN serotonin release is lowest in the late midday, peaks at the light-to-dark transition and falls through the night. It keeps this rhythm in constant darkness, so the clock itself sets the timing, not light. Serotonin arrives directly from the median raphe or relayed through the IGL from the dorsal raphe. During the subjective day it advances the clock a little: 8-OH-DPAT at CT6 moved mouse wheel-running about 37 minutes earlier, through 5-HT7 receptors. At night it gates light instead, cutting light-induced advances by 57 to 82 percent and, at a high dose, blocking delays.

LO5 · Explain how a steady supply of serotonin becomes a nightly melatonin signal.

In the pineal gland, AANAT acetylates serotonin into N-acetylserotonin, and a second enzyme methylates that into melatonin. The rhythm comes from regulating AANAT, not from the amount of serotonin, which is why Klein called it “the Timezyme”. The SCN sends the signal along a chain of neurons down the spinal cord, out to nerve cells in the neck and back up to the pineal, where norepinephrine is released at night. That raises cAMP, which switches AANAT on and protects it from breakdown; in rats AANAT mRNA rises more than 150-fold at night.

LO6 · Why is serotonin’s role in the clock still unsettled?

Results depend on species and method. In hamsters and mice, serotonergic drugs act as nonphotic signals that advance the clock by day; in rats they act more like light and shift it at night, the serotonergic paradox. Method matters too: serotonin on isolated mouse SCN slices (in vitro) advances the clock two to three hours, but 8-OH-DPAT in living mice (in vivo) gives only about 37 minutes. And mice that can’t make brain serotonin still keep a daily rhythm, though a changed one, so serotonin seems to be one of several signals that adjust the clock rather than the source of its rhythm.

LO7 · Compare the SCN with a local clock in the liver.

Both run on the same molecular clock. The SCN sits in the hypothalamus, directly above where the optic nerves cross, and is the master pacemaker. It takes light as its main cue, through the retinohypothalamic tract, synchronizes its many cell clocks with VIP and GABA, and helps regulate sleep, activity, temperature and hormones. A liver clock is a local clock: it times that tissue’s functions, such as processing nutrients and regulating glucose, and it responds to a mix of cues such as feeding, hormones, temperature and nervous-system signals. Signals from the SCN, along with feeding, help keep local clocks aligned.

LO9 · How strong is the evidence that clock genes drive feeding and activity rhythms?

It’s real but needs care. Clock mutant mice had weaker feeding rhythms and often became obese, and mice without functional Bmal1 had disrupted activity patterns. But the Clock mutation also changes metabolism, which could alter appetite on its own, and light can drive activity directly even when the clock isn’t working, so behavior has to be tracked over several days and conditions. Whole-body Bmal1 loss can also cause developmental problems, so Mieda and Sakurai removed it only from the nervous system. Those mice struggled to adjust activity and feeding, which suggests clocks outside the SCN help time food-related behavior.
