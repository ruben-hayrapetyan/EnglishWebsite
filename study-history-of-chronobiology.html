The chapter in 16 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Time-keeping throughout history

Cycles outside and inside the body

  • The sun's position, the seasons, sunshine duration and the moon phase are exogenous cycles: they come from outside you.
  • Without them, the body still runs internal endogenous cycles, built on cyclical processes such as metabolism, the sleep-wake cycle, the menstrual cycle and hormone release.
  • Many of these fall into three groups: ultradian, circadian and infradian rhythms. Studying them is chronobiology.

1 · Time-keeping throughout history

Early ways to keep time

  • Egyptians, Sumerians, Persians and Chinese used the lunar cycle for lunar calendars. Our month is still roughly one lunar cycle long.
  • Farming made people track the seasons.
  • Sundials, hourglasses, water-wheels and candle clocks measured smaller divisions of time, but each had drawbacks. A sundial needs a clear, sunny day and has limited precision.

1 · Time-keeping throughout history

The pendulum clock

  • Christiaan Huygens invented the pendulum clock in 1656.
  • It cut time error down to mere seconds per day, giving scientists the precision to measure tiny intervals of time.
  • It stayed the most effective form of time-keeping until the 1930s.

1 · Time-keeping throughout history

Atomic clocks and the second

  • Modern atomic clocks count oscillations of the cesium-133 atom, which happen at highly consistent intervals.
  • The SI second is a fixed number of these oscillations, so it can be measured anywhere in the universe and stays constant.
  • The legacy second, 1/86,400 of one Earth revolution, needs an observer near Earth, and Earth's revolution isn't constant.

2 · Ancient chronobiology

Rhythms noticed long before the field existed

  • Many ancient civilizations noticed the periodic nature of natural processes.
  • Many independently developed their own time-keeping systems and calendars.
  • Some also learned to use the body's internal rhythms for medicine.

2 · Ancient chronobiology

Egyptians: telling time by the stars

  • Around 3000 BCE, the Egyptians developed a lunar calendar.
  • 36 groups of stars called decans let them track time even at night.
  • They first split the year into 360 parts, then added days when they noticed the star Sirius reappeared every 365 days, creating the 365-day year.

2 · Ancient chronobiology

Mayans: two calendars

  • The Tzolk’in combined 20 named days with 13 numbers into a 260-day calendar, used mainly for religious ceremonies.
  • The civil calendar had 365 days: 18 months of 20 days plus 5 Uayed days. They estimated the year at about 365.24 days but added no leap year.
  • They used astronomical observations to time corn cultivation, an ancient use of chronobiological ideas.

2 · Ancient chronobiology

China: timing medicine to the body

  • Chinese physicians linked times of day to the activity of bodily functions, and timed treatments and predicted when symptoms would worsen.
  • The Yellow Emperor’s Classic of Medicine describes ‘Qi’ flowing through the body in two-hour intervals, starting with the lungs from 03:00 to 05:00.
  • Su Song’s Astronomical Clock Tower used a water-wheel to track time.

2 · Ancient chronobiology

Greece and Rome: critical days

  • Greek and Roman physicians saw that symptoms such as fevers came in regular patterns, and that the timing of treatment affected how well it worked.
  • They logged patterns on “critical days”, when a patient’s symptoms would either worsen or fade.
  • They used the sundial in sunlight and the clepsydra, a water clock, indoors and at night.

3 · Activity: engineering a sundial

How a sundial works

  • The sundial dates back to 1500 BCE in ancient Egypt and spread to the Greeks and Romans.
  • The gnomon casts a shadow onto the dial, which is marked with angles for different times.
  • The sun moves at a constant 15 degrees per hour, so the shadow moves and tells the time.

3 · Activity: engineering a sundial

Build one, then reflect

  • A paper plate is the dial and a straw through its center is the gnomon. Align the gnomon with True North (northern hemisphere) or True South (southern hemisphere).
  • At the start of every hour, mark the shadow's position and label it. The goal is accuracy to within 20 minutes.
  • Then ask: what does a sundial depend on, and how would you guess the time in a room without windows?

4 · A birth of a new field

De Mairan's mimosa in the dark

  • In 1729, French astronomer Jean-Jacques d’Ortous de Mairan put a mimosa plant in a dark cabinet.
  • A mimosa opens its leaves by day and folds them at night. In the dark, it kept opening and closing at regular intervals.
  • De Mairan concluded the plant could “sense the Sun without ever seeing it”. The finding became the catalyst for chronobiology.

4 · A birth of a new field

From plants to animals

  • Almost a century after de Mairan, Augustin Pyramus de Candolle measured the mimosa's leaf movements at a period of about 22 to 23 hours.
  • Colin Pittendrigh showed that fruit flies also keep a roughly 24-hour cycle in darkness, so these rhythms exist in animals too.
  • Changing when the flies saw light shifted their cycles forward or backward. Chronobiology became an experimental science in plants and animals.

5 · Chronobiology in the modern day

The period gene

  • By the 1960s, scientists knew biological rhythms existed but not what caused them.
  • In 1971, Ron Konopka and Seymour Benzer found fruit flies with longer, shorter or no circadian rhythms, and traced the variation to a gene they called period.
  • It was the first gene found to control a biological clock, proving rhythms are not learned or a passive response to stimuli. Its protein, PER, builds up at night and degrades by day.

5 · Chronobiology in the modern day

The transcription-translation feedback loop

  • A clock gene produces mRNA, which makes a clock protein. The protein builds up until the cell senses there is enough.
  • The proteins then break down, making room for more to be produced. This self-sustaining negative feedback loop takes roughly 24 hours.
  • Hall, Rosbash and Young discovered it and won the 2017 Nobel prize in medicine.

5 · Chronobiology in the modern day

Chronobiology today

  • Sleep disorders such as insomnia, non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder and shift-wake disorder are studied by asking what happens to the person's biological clocks.
  • Research on circadian disruption asks how jet-lag and night shifts affect the circadian rhythm and health.
  • Chronotherapy continues to develop an idea that goes back to ancient times.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Chronobiology
The scientific study of natural biological rhythms and how time affects living systems.
Endogenous cycle
Rhythms that originate from within an organism and persist even without external environmental cues like light or dark.
Exogenous cycle
Rhythms or behavioral shifts that are directly driven, forced, or influenced by external environmental factors.
Cyclical processes
A self-sustaining, repeating biological rhythm that regulates the timing and duration of physiological and behavioral activities in living organisms.
Ultradian rhythm
Biological cycles that operate on a cycle shorter than 24-hours.
Circadian rhythm
Biological cycles that operate on a roughly 24-hour cycle.
Infradian rhythm
Biological cycles that operate on a cycle longer than 24-hours.
Decans
36 groups of stars the ancient Egyptians used to track time even during night.
Tzolk’in
The Mayan calendar of 20 named days combined with 13 other numbers, creating a 260-day calendar used primarily for religious ceremonies.
Clepsydra
A device that uses water to measure time; the Greeks and Romans used it in rooms and at night-time.
Gnomon
The part of the sundial that casts the shadow onto the dial.
Dial
The part of the sundial lined with numerous different angles corresponding to different times.
Period (gene)
The first gene scientists discovered that controls a biological clock; it produces a protein called PER that builds up during the night and degrades in the day.
Transcription-translation feedback loop
A self-sustaining negative feedback loop in which a clock gene produces mRNA, which creates a clock protein that builds up and then breaks down, taking roughly 24 hours to complete.

LO1 · Compare how the Egyptians, the Mayans, the Chinese and the Greeks and Romans used time and rhythms.

The Egyptians and Mayans mostly built calendars from the sky. The Egyptians used 36 star groups called decans to tell time at night and moved from 360 to 365 days when they saw Sirius return every 365 days. The Mayans had a 260-day religious calendar and a 365-day civil calendar, estimated the year at about 365.24 days, and used their system to time corn cultivation. The Chinese and the Greeks and Romans applied rhythms to the body: Chinese physicians timed treatments to when bodily functions were active, and Greek and Roman physicians tracked fevers and "critical days", using sundials and the clepsydra to keep time.

LO2 · Walk a friend through building a sundial and explain why it works.

Poke a hole in the center of a paper plate, which is the dial, and push a straw through it as the gnomon, vertical or slightly slanted. On a sunny day, set it outside and use a compass to align the gnomon with True North in the northern hemisphere or True South in the southern hemisphere. At the start of every hour, mark where the shadow falls and label it. It works because the sun moves at a constant 15 degrees per hour, so the gnomon's shadow moves by a steady angle. Its limits: it needs a clear, sunny day and is only accurate to about 20 minutes.

LO3 · How does today's understanding of biological rhythms differ from what ancient physicians knew?

Ancient physicians observed patterns, like Qi moving through organs in two-hour intervals or fevers on "critical days", and timed treatments to them without knowing the cause. Modern chronobiology knows the cause: clock genes such as period. A clock gene makes mRNA and a clock protein that builds up and then breaks down in a self-sustaining feedback loop of about 24 hours. The period gene proved rhythms aren't learned or a passive response to stimuli. Time-keeping changed too, from sundials and water clocks to atomic clocks. The medical goal continues in chronotherapy and research on sleep disorders and circadian disruption.

LO4 · What themes kept pushing people to study time and biological rhythms, from ancient times to today?

Three threads recur. Agriculture: ancient cultures tracked the seasons, and the Mayans timed corn cultivation. Medicine: Chinese, Greek and Roman physicians timed treatments, and today chronobiology studies sleep disorders, circadian disruption and chronotherapy. Precision and experiment: better clocks, from the sundial to the pendulum clock and atomic clocks, let scientists measure tiny intervals, and experiments like de Mairan's mimosa in the dark, Pittendrigh's light-shifted fruit flies and the discovery of the period gene turned observations into a science.
