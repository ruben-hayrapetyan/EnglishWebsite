The chapter in 15 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · Introduction and background of chronotypes

What a chronotype is

  • A chronotype is "a biological surrogate for an individual's phase of entrainment."
  • In other words, it is a measurable proxy of how your internal clock aligns with external factors (Roenneberg, 2012).
  • It affects preferred bedtime, preferred wake-up time, and even productivity and health.

1 · Introduction and background of chronotypes

Why chronotypes evolved: the sentinel hypothesis

  • Sleep is a state of vulnerability to predators, so a group needs at least one member awake.
  • The sentinel hypothesis: group animals have asynchronous sleep times so someone stays attentive to the surroundings.
  • Samson et al. (2017) found that 99.8% of tribe members in a hunter-gatherer group "were awake between the time the first member went to sleep and the last woke up."
  • So chronotype differences may come from natural selection, as a group defense.

2 · The biological mechanism behind differences in chronotypes

From early observations to a biological idea

  • In 1900, O'Shea described differences in peak intellectual and physical timings in Aspects of Economy.
  • Chronotypes were only established as biological mechanisms in 1976, by Horne & Ostbërg.
  • Their aim was to revise the concepts of morningness and eveningness.

2 · The biological mechanism behind differences in chronotypes

The 19-question questionnaire

  • A multiple-choice questionnaire about sleep preferences, such as "When you have no commitments the next day, what time do you go to bed compared to your usual bedtime?"
  • Each answer has a score, and the scores add up to a total.
  • A lower total means an evening preference; a higher total means a morning preference.

2 · The biological mechanism behind differences in chronotypes

Three types, checked against body temperature

  • Participants were grouped into 3 categories: morning, intermediate and evening type.
  • Their questionnaire results correlated directly with body temperature.
  • That link to a body measure validated the science of chronotypes.

2 · The biological mechanism behind differences in chronotypes

Morning types start the day warmer

  • From 9am, morning types had a higher body temperature than evening types.
  • They stayed at the upper end until about 8pm.
  • There the two trendlines intersected and the temperature dynamic reversed.

2 · The biological mechanism behind differences in chronotypes

Both peak in the evening, at different times

  • Morning types peaked at 7:32PM; evening types at 8:40PM.
  • Morning types: higher temperature to start the day, then an earlier peak and decline.
  • Evening types: lower temperature to start the day, then a later peak and decline.
  • Replications consistently found the same pattern.

2 · The biological mechanism behind differences in chronotypes

A lasting standard

  • Horne and Ostbërg's research paved the way for more chronotype research.
  • Their 19-item questionnaire became a standard for assigning participants to chronotype groups.
  • Morning, intermediate and evening are still the most relevant categories today.

3 · Chronotypes and physical performance

Warmer muscles perform better

  • For every 1 degree Celsius increase in body temperature, performance increases by about 3.5% (Wilson et al., 2025).
  • Higher body temperature means higher muscle temperature, because blood carries core heat to the skeletal tissues.
  • So baseline body temperature may directly affect an athlete's performance.

3 · Chronotypes and physical performance

A possible link, not a proven one

  • Chronotypes differ in the timing of body temperature, which could in turn affect exercise.
  • But research in this field is limited, and causation cannot be fully established.
  • Time-of-day performance differences between chronotypes are still only speculation.

4 · Chronotypes, social jetlag, and mental disorders

Social jetlag

  • Social jetlag is the gap between your preferred sleep schedule and your actual one, caused by work, school or family.
  • The most common U.S. workday starts at 8AM (Torpey, 2015).
  • Other countries differ only marginally: 8:30AM in Japan, 9AM in China.

4 · Chronotypes, social jetlag, and mental disorders

Evening types and mental health

  • A narrative review (Zou et al., 2022) found evening types more likely to have mental disorders; being a morning type is protective.
  • A Dutch correlational study linked the evening type to worse morning mood and to major depressive disorder (Antypa et al.).

4 · Chronotypes, social jetlag, and mental disorders

Sleep deprivation as the explanation

  • Compared with morning types, evening types are 247% and intermediate types 61% more likely to have insomnia.
  • Evening types' delayed rhythms make a misaligned schedule harder to adjust to, inducing insomnia-like symptoms.
  • So circadian misalignment (social jetlag) puts evening types at greater risk of physical, mental and social issues.

5 · How do chronotypes affect responses to work schedules?

The case of Patient F

  • Patient F's chronotype affected how she responded to a schedule change in her life.
  • Her story does not establish causation between chronotypes and mental illnesses.
  • It does link chronotype to sleep, mood and daily functioning.

6 · Conclusion

Never compromise sleep

  • Chronotype relates to body temperature, responses to schedule changes, physical performance at specific times, and sleep.
  • Open question: do early work start times and night shifts benefit society, or degrade individual health?
  • The key takeaway: all chronotype-related issues arise from sleep, so always get a good night's sleep.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Chronotypes
A biological surrogate for an individual's phase of entrainment.
Morningness
Those who show extreme preferences for daytime activity; in these individuals, peak performance and alertness is associated with the early-morning hours.
Eveningness
Extreme preferences for nighttime activities; in these individuals, heightened alertness and peak performance is linked to the evening hours.
Social jetlag
The difference between an individual's preferred sleep schedule and their actual sleep schedule that is caused by societal factors such as work, school, or family.
Major depressive disorder
A common and serious mental health condition defined by a persistent low mood and a loss of interest or pleasure in daily activities.
Insomnia
The condition of difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or having good quality sleep.
Sentinel hypothesis
Group animals have asynchronous sleep times because at least one person would need to stay attentive to the surroundings.
Horne and Ostbërg's questionnaire
A 19-question multiple choice questionnaire about sleep preferences; a lower total score indicates an evening preference and a higher score a morning preference.
Morning type
Has higher body temperatures to start the day, along with an earlier temperature peak and decline.
Evening type
Has lower body temperatures to start the day, but a peak and decline in body temperature later in the evening.

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 · What are the 3 main chronotypes, where might they have come from, and how do they affect someone's life?

A chronotype is a measurable proxy of how your internal clock aligns with external factors. Horne and Ostbërg's 19-question questionnaire sorts people into morning, intermediate and evening types, which are still the main categories. They may have evolved through natural selection: under the sentinel hypothesis, staggered sleep keeps someone awake to guard the group. Chronotype affects preferred bedtime and wake-up time, productivity and health. Evening types, for example, are more likely to have insomnia and mental disorders when schedules don't fit them.

LO2 · Describe how body temperature changes across the day for morning and evening types.

Morning types start the day with a higher body temperature and stay warmer until about 8pm, when the two curves cross and the pattern reverses. Both types peak in the evening, but morning types peak earlier (7:32PM) and decline earlier, while evening types start cooler and peak later (8:40PM). Replications have consistently found this pattern, and it is what showed that the questionnaire's chronotypes are biological.

LO3 · Which chronotype do you think you are, and how has it shaped your mood, daily functioning and habits?

A strong answer uses the chapter's tools. For example: "On free days I go to bed much later than usual and I'm not tired at 11:00 PM, so I'd score low on Horne and Ostbërg's questionnaire, which points to an evening type. With 8AM classes I have social jetlag: I sleep less, my mood is worse in the morning, and I've had trouble falling asleep, which matches the higher insomnia risk the chapter describes for evening types." A morning type would instead describe feeling best early and less strain from early starts.

LO4 · Propose a solution for someone whose schedule doesn't match their chronotype, and explain why it should help.

The harm comes from circadian misalignment, or social jetlag: the gap between preferred and actual sleep schedules. Evening types' delayed rhythms make that gap hard to adjust to, causing insomnia-like symptoms that are linked to worse mood and mental disorders. So a solution should shrink the gap, for example later work or school start times for evening types, or avoiding schedule changes that clash with a person's chronotype, as in Patient F's case. Above all, it should protect sleep, because the chapter says all chronotype-related issues arise from sleep.
