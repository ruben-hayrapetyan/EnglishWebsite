The chapter in 14 slides

Each slide is one idea from the chapter, in order.

1 · The immune system

The innate system: barriers and chemicals

  • The innate immune system is a general, fast-acting first line of defense against any pathogen. It works through physical, chemical and cellular means.
  • Physical barriers keep pathogens out: nasal mucus traps microbes, and the skin’s dry, slightly acidic surface makes it hard for bacteria to colonize.
  • Chemical defenses such as stomach acid, tears and sweat kill pathogens that reach the body’s surfaces and openings. Stomach acid kills pathogens swallowed with food or drinks.

1 · The immune system

Innate cells, and their limits

  • Cellular defenses use cells like macrophages and neutrophils to engulf and destroy pathogens.
  • These defenses are fast but non-specific, and they have no memory.
  • Some pathogens have evolved ways to bypass them. For these more complex infections, the adaptive immune system is needed.

1 · The immune system

The adaptive system: slower, targeted, remembers

  • The adaptive immune system gives a slower but much more targeted response to specific pathogens.
  • On first exposure it can take several days to develop.
  • It retains information about the pathogen for future attacks. That is why vaccines inject a harmless form of a pathogen: so the adaptive system can develop immunity to it.

1 · The immune system

One response, start to finish

  • When a threat is recognized, innate immune cells release cytokines, signaling proteins that coordinate the response and alert other immune cells.
  • Nearby blood vessels widen so innate cells reach the site quickly, which also causes redness, warmth and swelling.
  • Antibodies and immune cells then neutralize or destroy the remaining pathogens and infected cells. Afterwards, anti-inflammatory signals, including cortisol, dampen the response so tissue can repair.

2 · Circadian rhythms in the immune system

Not on high alert all the time

  • Staying on high alert all the time would be too taxing for the body.
  • So the immune system surges at night and is less active during the day.
  • Much of this nighttime surge comes from cytokines.

2 · Circadian rhythms in the immune system

Cytokines, symptoms and cortisol

  • Some cytokines are pro-inflammatory, others reduce inflammation, and some raise the body’s internal temperature.
  • They can be linked, though not one-to-one, to fever, aches, tiredness and inflammation.
  • Cytokines are balanced by cortisol, a hormone that suppresses immune activity. Cortisol is lowest in the first half of the night and highest around waking.

2 · Circadian rhythms in the immune system

Petrovsky and Harrison (1998): the setup

  • Blood samples were taken from participants at intervals throughout the day.
  • The blood’s immune response was activated with bacterial endotoxin (the outer shells of bacteria) and tetanus toxoid (a harmless form of tetanus).
  • Cytokine counts showed how active the immune system was; plasma cortisol showed how much it was suppressed.

2 · Circadian rhythms in the immune system

Which rhythm drives which?

  • Cytokine production surged at night and in the early morning, inversely to plasma cortisol.
  • An inverse pattern alone can’t show which drives the other. Raising cortisol artificially in the evening dulled cytokine levels: the cytokine rhythm depends on the cortisol rhythm.
  • These nighttime cytokine peaks help explain nighttime flares, when symptoms worsen at night and in the early morning.

2 · Circadian rhythms in the immune system

Vaccine timing: Long et al. (2016)

  • A vaccine’s success depends on how strongly the immune system responds, so the time it is given matters, especially for older adults, who tend to respond more weakly.
  • Patients over 65 in UK general practices gave blood at their flu shot and one month later. Those vaccinated in the morning had higher antibody levels than those vaccinated in the afternoon.
  • Cytokine levels did not account for the difference. The cause is still open research, but the response clearly depends on the time of day.

2 · Circadian rhythms in the immune system

Sepsis and time of day

  • Against a large enough threat, the immune system can overreact in a poorly controlled way and damage the body’s own cells: sepsis.
  • Halberg et al. (1960) injected mice with bacterial endotoxin at different times to drive them into sepsis.
  • Mice injected during the day, their rest phase, when their cortisol was lowest, were at much higher risk of an overreaction than mice injected at night.

3 · Seasonal rhythms in immunity

A yearly rhythm, through gene expression

  • Over a year, the immune system also follows a longer, infradian rhythm that tracks the seasons.
  • Gene expression is how the body controls protein production: when a gene is active, its protein can be produced.
  • Immune cells and proteins, such as cytokines, depend on certain genes being active, so seasonal shifts in gene expression change how the immune system works.

3 · Seasonal rhythms in immunity

Dopico et al. (2015): seasonal genes

  • They asked whether genes like BMAL1, known to follow a circadian rhythm, also follow a seasonal rhythm.
  • Instead of running an experiment, they analyzed large existing datasets of blood samples from around the world. About a quarter of the tested genes changed their expression seasonally.
  • The shifts were reversed between the northern and southern hemispheres, so gene expression matched local seasons.

3 · Seasonal rhythms in immunity

A more reactive winter

  • In winter, immune-related genes shifted toward a more pro-inflammatory state.
  • That meant more monocytes, lower activity of BMAL1 (which suppresses inflammation), and more of the proteins made in response to cytokine firing.
  • The body may have evolved this to prepare for the higher number of infections in winter.

3 · Seasonal rhythms in immunity

Liu and Taioli (2015): blood counts by season

  • CDC survey data with blood counts from 1999 to 2012 showed white blood cells and neutrophils were higher in winter and spring than in summer and fall.
  • The difference was stronger in children than adults, perhaps because a developing immune system is more sensitive to seasonal changes.
  • A more inflammatory winter state may help defend against that season’s colds and flu. The immune system depends on time of day and time of year.

Key terms

Try to recall each answer before you flip. Terms you mark "Still learning" come back sooner, and your progress stays in this browser.

Cytokine
A protein released by immune cells that coordinate inflammation and the body’s response to a potentially harmful infection.
Antibodies
Proteins made by B-cells which bind to specific antigens and mark them for destruction.
Sepsis
A state in which your body’s immune system overreacts to an infection and starts harming your own cells. This is life threatening in most cases.
Neutrophil
A very common type of white blood cell which is a first-responder to infections and destroys pathogens by absorbing them.
Plasma cortisol
The concentration of the stress hormone cortisol in blood. This suppresses immune activity and cytokine production.
Innate immune system
A general, fast-acting first line of defense against any pathogen, operating through physical, chemical and cellular mediums.
Adaptive immune system
A slower but much more targeted response to specific pathogens that retains information about the pathogen for future similar attacks.
Nighttime flares
A common phenomenon in which one’s symptoms worsen during the night and in the early morning.
Gene expression
How the body controls protein production, reducing the production of some proteins while amplifying the production of others.
Monocyte
The white blood cell involved in inflammation.

  1. Someone drinks water containing bacteria. Which innate defense most directly deals with those swallowed bacteria?

    1. Mucus in the nose, which traps microbes
    2. Stomach acid, which kills pathogens swallowed with food or drinks
    3. Antibodies left over from an earlier vaccination
    4. The skin’s dry, slightly acidic surface
    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Nasal mucus is a physical barrier for the nasal passages, not for what is swallowed.
    2. Right: stomach acid is a chemical defense that specifically kills pathogens swallowed with food or drinks.
    3. Antibodies belong to the adaptive immune system, not the innate one, and they target specific pathogens.
    4. The skin stops bacteria colonizing the body’s surface; it doesn’t act on swallowed bacteria.

    The innate system works through physical barriers that keep pathogens out (mucus, skin), chemical defenses that kill pathogens at the body’s surfaces and openings (stomach acid, tears, sweat), and cells that engulf pathogens.

  2. The innate immune system is fast. Why does the body still need an adaptive immune system?

    1. The adaptive system responds faster on first exposure.
    2. Innate defenses only work against pathogens that are swallowed.
    3. Innate defenses are non-specific with no memory, and some pathogens have evolved ways to bypass them.
    4. Innate defenses only work at night, when the immune system surges.
    Show the answer

    Answer: C

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. The reverse: the adaptive system is slower and can take several days to develop on first exposure.
    2. Innate defenses include skin, mucus, tears, sweat and cells, not just stomach acid.
    3. Right: the chapter says these limits are why more complex infections need the adaptive system.
    4. The nighttime surge is a change in activity, not an on/off switch for innate defenses.

    The innate system trades precision for speed. The adaptive system is slower but targeted, and it remembers a pathogen for future attacks.

  3. Which statement best explains how a vaccine protects someone?

    1. It thickens the skin and mucus barriers so pathogens can’t get in.
    2. It kills pathogens directly, the way stomach acid does.
    3. It keeps cytokine levels permanently high.
    4. It gives a harmless form of a pathogen so the adaptive immune system can develop immunity and remember it.
    Show the answer

    Answer: D

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Vaccines train the adaptive system; they don’t change the innate physical barriers.
    2. That describes a chemical innate defense, not a vaccine.
    3. Vaccines don’t keep cytokines high, and the chapter says the immune system can’t stay on high alert all the time.
    4. Right: vaccines rely on the adaptive system retaining information about a pathogen.

    Because the adaptive immune system retains information about a pathogen, vaccines inject a harmless form of it so the body is ready for the real thing.

  4. An infected cut turns red, warm and swollen. According to the chapter, what causes this?

    1. Nearby blood vessels widen so fast-acting innate cells reach the site quickly.
    2. Cortisol rises at the site to dampen the immune response.
    3. Antibodies attack the healthy tissue around the cut.
    4. The skin’s acidic surface reacts with the bacteria.
    Show the answer

    Answer: A

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Right: widening blood vessels speed innate cells to the infection and also cause redness, warmth and swelling.
    2. Cortisol is an anti-inflammatory signal that comes after the attack and dampens the response; it doesn’t cause swelling.
    3. Antibodies target remaining pathogens and infected cells; attacking the body’s own cells describes an overreaction like sepsis.
    4. The skin’s surface stops bacteria colonizing; it isn’t the source of inflammation.

    In a joint response, innate cells release cytokines to the threat, blood vessels widen, then antibodies and immune cells finish the targeted attack before cortisol and other signals let tissue repair.

    Show the answer

    Answer: B

    Why each option is right or wrong:

    1. Cortisol suppresses immune activity, so high cortisol goes with lower cytokines, not higher.
    2. Right: cytokine production surged at night, inversely to plasma cortisol, which is lowest in the first half of the night.
    3. The study found a clear daily pattern in cytokine production.
    4. Cortisol peaks around waking, not at midnight, and high cortisol would dull cytokines.

    Cytokine production surged at night and in the early morning, running opposite to the circadian rhythm of plasma cortisol.

  6. Why did Petrovsky and Harrison artificially raise plasma cortisol in the evening?

    1. To drive the blood samples into a state like sepsis.

Explain it back

Answer each prompt in your own words, out loud or on paper, before you open the model answer.

LO1 · Describe the two parts of the immune system and how they work together when a pathogen gets in.

The innate system is a fast, general first line of defense: physical barriers like skin and mucus, chemical defenses like stomach acid, tears and sweat, and cells like macrophages and neutrophils that engulf pathogens. It is non-specific and has no memory. The adaptive system is slower but targeted, and it remembers pathogens, which is why vaccines work. In a response, innate cells release cytokines, blood vessels widen (redness, warmth, swelling), antibodies and immune cells destroy what remains, and then signals like cortisol dampen the response so tissue can repair.

LO2 · Why is the immune system more active at night, and how do we know cortisol drives the pattern?

Being on high alert all the time would be too taxing, so the immune system surges at night, mostly through cytokines. Cortisol suppresses immune activity and is lowest in the first half of the night. Petrovsky and Harrison found cytokine production surged at night and in the early morning, opposite to plasma cortisol. Since an inverse pattern can’t show cause, they raised cortisol in the evening and cytokines were dulled, so the cytokine rhythm depends on the cortisol rhythm. This helps explain nighttime flares. Vaccine timing matters too: Long et al. found morning flu shots gave older adults higher antibody levels, though not because of cytokines.

LO3 · Explain how the time of exposure changed the risk of sepsis in Halberg’s mice.

Sepsis is when the immune system overreacts to a large threat in a poorly controlled way and damages the body’s own cells. Halberg et al. injected mice with bacterial endotoxin at different times. Mice injected during the day, their rest phase, when cortisol was lowest, were at much higher risk of overreacting than mice injected at night. With less cortisol suppressing the immune system, the same threat was more dangerous.

LO4 · How does the immune system change across the year, and how might that affect people?

It follows an infradian, seasonal rhythm. Dopico et al. found about a quarter of tested genes changed expression with the seasons, reversed between hemispheres. In winter, immune genes shifted toward a pro-inflammatory state: more monocytes, lower BMAL1 activity and more proteins made in response to cytokines. Liu and Taioli found white blood cells and neutrophils higher in winter and spring, especially in children. A more reactive winter immune system may help defend against that season’s colds and flu.
